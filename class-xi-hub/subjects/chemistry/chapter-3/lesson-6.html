Ionic Character & Dipole Moment

Chapter 3 · Lesson 6 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 13–15 min read Topics 3.3.3 & 3.3.4

🎯 Why Should You Care?

You have been taught that bonds are either ionic or covalent. That is a convenient lie. Almost every real bond sits somewhere in between.

When two different atoms share electrons, the greedier one pulls harder and ends up slightly negative while its partner goes slightly positive. The bond is still covalent, but it has picked up some ionic character.

Then comes the twist that catches everyone out. H2O and CO2 both contain polar bonds — yet water is polar and CO2 is not. The bonds are not what decides it. The shape is.

⚡ Ionic Character of a Covalent Bond

📚 What happens

When two different atoms having different electronegativities unite through a covalent bond, a partially positive charge is produced on the less electronegative atom and a partially negative charge on the highly electronegative atom.

The molecule is now said to be polar, bearing ionic character on its two oppositely charged poles, and the bond between the two atoms is known as a polar covalent bond.

The classic example — hydrogen chloride

The electronegativity of hydrogen (2.1) is less than that of chlorine (3.0). Therefore the shared electron pair is more shifted towards the chlorine atom, which results in an ionic character appearing on the covalent molecule.

                                    +δ      -δ
        H  ×⌢  Cl      ──────→      H ───── Cl

     electrons shared but          chlorine pulls harder,
     pulled towards Cl             so poles develop
A polar covalent bond in HCl

What decides how ionic a bond is

Since the ionic character of a covalent bond mainly depends upon the electronegativity difference between the two joined atoms, the strength of a polar bond can be attributed by taking the E.N difference as a primary tool. However, the size of the atom and bond length are also influencing factors for bond strength.

The electronegativities of some common elements in their relative order are given as:

H < I < C & S < Br < N & Cl < O < F
(2.1)  (2.4)  (2.5)   (2.8)   (3.0)   (3.5)  (4.0)

When the difference of E.N of the two atoms is almost the same, then the relative size of the atoms decides the strength of ionic character.

🧭 Dipole Moment

We learnt in the previous section that when a molecule is assembled by two atoms of different electronegativities, a partially positive and negative charge is created on the two ends. The net magnitude of this positive and negative charge in a diatomic molecule is equal, and thus the molecule is electrically neutral and said to be a dipole.

📚 Definition

Dipole moment is a measuring scale used for the degree of polarity of a polar bond. It is defined as "the product of the magnitude of charge and the distance between the positive and negative centres".

Dipole moment = q · d

The net molecular moment is indicated by an arrow (→), since dipole moment is a vector quantity.

Units

It is noted experimentally that the charge (q) is of the order of 10−10 esu and the distance between the positive and negative centre (d) is in the range of 10−8 cm. Therefore the dipole moment of all polar molecules is found in the limit of 10−18 esu.cm. This quantity is known as the Debye (D), after the name of its introducer, Peter Debye.

1 Debye = 1 × 10−18 esu.cm

The S.I unit of dipole moment is the Coulomb metre (Cm). The interrelation between Debye and coulomb metre is written as:

1 D = 3.336 × 10−30 Cm

🔺 Molecular Polarity and the Shape of Molecules

The dipole moment of a molecule depends not only upon the polarity of its bonds, but also upon the geometry of the molecule.

For example, H2O and CO2 both contain polar bonds, but:

  • The angular structure of H2O results in a dipole moment of 1.85 D, and it is said to be a polar molecule.
  • CO2 on the other hand has zero dipole moment. Although both C=O bonds in CO2 are polar, they are directed equally on opposite sides at a 180° angle making a linear geometry, thus the molecule is non-polar.

Why "vector quantity" is the whole point

Bond dipoles have direction, not just size. In a symmetrical molecule they point away from each other and cancel to zero. In an unsymmetrical one they partly add up and a net dipole survives. That is why you must know the shape (Lesson 3) before you can predict polarity.

Look at the zeros

CO2, CS2, CH4 and H2 all have μ = 0. Three of them contain polar bonds — but all four are symmetrical (linear or tetrahedral), so the bond dipoles cancel. Symmetry is the giveaway.

🧮 Solved Self-Assessment (from the book)

Self Assessment Compare the bond strengths of (i) sigma and pi bond (ii) polar and non-polar molecule.
(i) Sigma vs pi bond

A sigma bond is stronger than a pi bond. Sigma is formed by head to head overlapping, giving maximum overlap with the electron cloud concentrated between the two nuclei. A pi bond is formed by lateral overlapping of parallel p-orbitals, where the region of overlap is small and the cloud is split above and below the axis. Since bond strength is proportional to the extent of overlap, sigma > pi.

(ii) Polar vs non-polar molecule

A polar (hetero-diatomic) molecule has the stronger bond. The ionic character arising from the electronegativity difference makes the atoms lie closer together, which shortens the bond and raises the bond energy.

Evidence from Lesson 5: HCl (polar) = 431 kJ/mol vs Cl2 (non-polar) = 242 kJ/mol; and H–Cl (1.27 Å) is shorter than Cl–Cl (1.99 Å).

Self Assessment Which of the following molecules have a dipole moment? In each case give a reason for your answer.  CHCl3, SCl2, H2O, CO2
CHCl3 — YES, it has a dipole moment

Chloroform is tetrahedral, but the four attached atoms are not all the same — three chlorines and one hydrogen. The C–Cl bonds are much more polar than the C–H bond, so the molecule is unsymmetrical and the bond dipoles do not cancel. A net dipole moment survives.

SCl2 — YES, it has a dipole moment

Sulphur has two bond pairs and two lone pairs (AX2E2), so the molecule is angular like water. The two polar S–Cl bond dipoles point in directions that do not cancel, so there is a net dipole moment.

H2O — YES, it has a dipole moment

Water is angular (104.5°). The two polar O–H bond dipoles add together rather than cancelling, giving μ = 1.85 D.

CO2 — NO, its dipole moment is zero

Both C=O bonds are polar, but CO2 is linear, so the two bond dipoles are equal in magnitude and directed exactly opposite at 180°. They cancel completely, giving μ = 0 — a non-polar molecule.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Tug of war 🪢 — Two equally strong teams pulling in opposite directions: the rope does not move (CO2, μ = 0). Two teams pulling at an angle: the rope moves off sideways (H2O, μ = 1.85 D). Same effort, different geometry, different result.
  2. A greedy sibling 🍫 — Share chocolate with someone greedier and they end up holding most of it. Chlorine (EN 3.0) is greedier than hydrogen (EN 2.1), so it holds the shared pair and gains a partial negative charge.
  3. Sliding scale, not a switch 🎚️ — Bonds are not simply "ionic" or "covalent". Cl–Cl is 0% ionic, C–H 4%, H–Cl 17%, H–F 43%. Every bond sits somewhere on that scale.
  4. Pulling a trolley 🛒 — Four people pushing a trolley from four symmetrical sides go nowhere (CH4, μ = 0). Remove one and it lurches off (CHCl3, μ ≠ 0). Symmetry cancels; asymmetry doesn't.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "If a molecule has polar bonds, the molecule must be polar."

❌ This is the single most important trap in the chapter. CO2, CS2 and CH4 all have polar bonds but zero dipole moment, because their symmetrical geometry makes the bond dipoles cancel. Always check the shape first.

⚠️ "A polar molecule carries a real charge."

❌ A dipole is electrically neutral overall — the positive and negative magnitudes are equal. Only the separation of charge makes it a dipole, not an excess of charge.

⚠️ "1 D = 3.33 × 10−19 Cm."

❌ 1 D = 3.336 × 10−30 Cm, and 1 Debye = 1 × 10−18 esu.cm. The exercise MCQ tests exactly this, with 1.6 × 10−19 put there as a decoy (that is the electron's charge, from Chapter 2).

⚠️ "Ionic character means the bond has become ionic."

❌ The bond is still a covalent bond — a polar covalent bond. Even H–F, the most ionic in Table 3.7, is only 43% ionic. It has not turned into an ionic compound.

⚠️ "Dipole moment is just a number."

❌ It is a vector quantity with direction, indicated by an arrow (→). That is the only reason opposing dipoles can cancel to zero.

Short Questions

Q6. The dipole moment of water is 1.85 D but CO2 has zero dipole moment. Why?

Answer: Because dipole moment depends not only on bond polarity but also on the geometry of the molecule. Water is angular, so its two polar O–H bond dipoles do not cancel — they combine to give a net 1.85 D. CO2 is linear; although both C=O bonds are polar, they are equal and directed oppositely at 180°, so they cancel completely and μ = 0.

Q7. Why does HF have greater ionic character than HCl?

Answer: Because ionic character depends mainly on the electronegativity difference between the bonded atoms. For H–F the difference is 1.9 (F = 4.0, H = 2.1), giving 43% ionic character. For H–Cl it is only 0.9 (Cl = 3.0), giving 17%. The larger the E.N difference, the more the shared pair shifts towards the electronegative atom and the greater the ionic character.

Q8. Define dipole moment and give its units.

Answer: Dipole moment is "the product of the magnitude of charge and the distance between the positive and negative centres", i.e. μ = q · d. It is a measure of the degree of polarity of a polar bond and is a vector quantity. Units: Debye (D), where 1 D = 1 × 10−18 esu.cm; the S.I unit is the Coulomb metre (Cm), with 1 D = 3.336 × 10−30 Cm.

Q9. Why does Cl–Cl have zero ionic character?

Answer: Because both atoms are identical, so their electronegativity difference is 0. Neither atom pulls the shared electron pair harder than the other, so no partial charges develop and there is no ionic character — it is a purely covalent (non-polar) bond.

📝 Summary

6 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. When atoms of different electronegativities bond covalently, the less electronegative atom gets +δ and the more electronegative one −δ. The bond is a polar covalent bond with ionic character.
  2. Ionic character depends mainly on the E.N difference (size and bond length also matter). E.N order: H(2.1) < I(2.4) < C&S(2.5) < Br(2.8) < N&Cl(3.0) < O(3.5) < F(4.0).
  3. Table 3.7: Cl–Cl 0% · C–H 4% · H–I 6% · C–Cl 10% · H–Br 12% · H–Cl 17% · O–H 39% · H–F 43%.
  4. Dipole moment = q · d — the product of charge magnitude and the distance between positive and negative centres. It is a vector quantity.
  5. Units: 1 Debye = 1 × 10−18 esu.cm; S.I unit Coulomb metre, with 1 D = 3.336 × 10−30 Cm. Named after Peter Debye.
  6. Shape decides molecular polarity. H2O is angular → μ = 1.85 D (polar). CO2 is linear → the equal, opposite bond dipoles cancel → μ = 0 (non-polar). Also zero: CS2, CH4, H2.

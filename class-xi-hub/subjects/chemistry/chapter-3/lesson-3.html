VSEPR Theory & Shapes of Molecules

Chapter 3 · Lesson 3 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 15–17 min read Topic 3.2.1

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Water is bent. Carbon dioxide is straight. Both are one central atom with two atoms attached — so why do they look completely different?

That difference is not a detail. It is why water is a liquid you can drink and CO2 is a gas you breathe out. Shape decides polarity, and polarity decides almost every physical property (as you will see in Lessons 6 and 7).

VSEPR is the theory that predicts shape, and the beautiful part is how simple it is: count the electron pairs around the central atom, then push them as far apart as possible. That is genuinely the whole method.

📖 Two Theories for Shape

Simple polyatomic molecules and ions generally acquire linear, tetrahedral, pyramidal and angular shapes etc. These shapes can be determined experimentally, however they can also be predicted on a theoretical basis.

Besides VBT there are two more significant theories which describe the shape of a molecule:

  1. Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory (VSEPR) — this lesson
  2. Hybridization — Lesson 4
📚 Who and when

This approach to the structure of a covalent molecule is due to Sidgwick and Powell (1940). They pointed out that the shapes of molecules can be determined by the repulsion between the electron pairs present in the valence shell of the central atom.

📜 The Six Postulates of VSEPR

📚 Postulate 1 — two kinds of electron pairs

There may be two types of electron pairs surrounding the central atom.

(a) Bond Pairs: These are the result of the sharing of unpaired electrons of the central atom with unpaired electrons of surrounding atoms. These are also called the active set of electrons.

(b) Lone Pairs: These are the paired electrons of the central atom which do not take part in sharing. They are also called non-bonding pairs. They are also considered to be an active set of electrons.

📚 Postulate 2 — they all repel

Being similarly charged (i.e. negative), the bond pairs as well as the lone pairs repel each other.

📚 Postulate 3 — as far apart as possible

Due to repulsion, the electron pairs of the central atom try to be as far apart as possible; hence they orient themselves in space in such a manner that the force of repulsion between them is minimized.

📚 Postulate 4 — the repulsion order

The force of repulsion between lone pairs and bond pairs is not the same. The order of repulsion is as follows:

Lone pair–Lone pair > Lone pair–Bond pair > Bond pair–Bond pair
📚 Postulate 5 — ignore pi electrons

In the case of molecules with double and triple bonds, the π electron pairs are not considered to be an active set of electrons, hence they are not included in the count of total electron pairs.

📚 Postulate 6 — shape follows the count

The shape of a molecule depends upon the total number of electron pairs (bonding and lone pairs).

💡
Do You Know?

A multiple bond (double and triple) is treated as a single bond in VSEPR, because π bonds do not alter the basic idealized geometry of a molecule. This is why CO2, with two double bonds, still counts as just two active electron pairs.

🔑
Postulate 4 is the one that explains every "odd" angle

If all pairs repelled equally, every 4-pair molecule would be a perfect 109.5° tetrahedron. But lone pairs repel harder, so they squeeze the bond angles down: 109.5° → 107° in NH3 (one lone pair) and → 104.5° in H2O (two lone pairs). One lone pair, one squeeze.

🔷 Prediction of Five Basic Shapes by VSEPR

1. Linear Shape (AX2)

This shape is found in AX2 type molecules in which the central atom (A) contains two bond pairs only. The equal repulsion of these two bond pairs extends the corner atoms (X) at a 180° angle and makes a linear shape.

Examples: BeCl2, C2H2, CO2.

              180°
     X ─────── A ─────── X
Linear — two bond pairs, no lone pairs

2. Trigonal Planar Shape (AX3)

This shape is found in AX3 type molecules where the central atom (A) possesses only three bond pairs of electrons. The equal repulsion of these bond pair electrons orients the three corner atoms (X) at 120°, and the geometry thus developed is trigonal planar.

Examples: BF3, C2H4, AlCl3.

          X           X
            ╲  120° ╱
             ╲     ╱
                A
                │  120°
                │
                X
Trigonal planar — three bond pairs, all in one plane

3. Tetrahedral Shape (AX4)

Molecules which have a tetrahedral shape are categorized into AX4 type. Here the central atom (A) contains four paired electrons. The repulsion of these four electron pairs directs the four X atoms towards the corners of a tetrahedron. The angle settled in this type of repulsion is 109.5°.

Examples: CH4, C2H6.

                X
                │
                │
                A ⋯⋯⋯⋯ X
              ╱   ╲
            ╱  109.5°
          X        X
Tetrahedral — four bond pairs, no lone pairs

4. Trigonal Pyramidal Shape (AX3E)

This is a cone-like shape with a tetrahedral base, found in molecules of the AX3E type, where A is the central atom, X are the corner atoms and E is the lone pair of electrons on the central atom.

The central atom in this type of molecule is surrounded by three bond pairs and one lone pair of its own. Since the repulsion of the lone pair of electrons is more effective than bond pair electrons, a slight distortion in the tetrahedral shape occurs by narrowing the angle at one side from 109.5° to 107°. It makes a pyramidal shape.

Examples: NH3, PH3.

              ●● ← lone pair (E) pushes down hard
              │
              A
            ╱ │ ╲
          ╱  107° ╲
        X     X     X
Trigonal pyramidal — three bond pairs + one lone pair

5. Angular (Bent) Shape (AX2E2)

Common examples of molecules having an angular shape are H2O, H2S etc. This is an AX2E2 type shape, in which A is the central atom, X are two corner atoms and E2 are two lone pairs of electrons present on the central atom.

There are two bond pairs and two lone pair electrons around the central atom, which initially makes a tetrahedral geometry in which two ends of the tetrahedron are filled with the two lone pairs and the other two corners occupied by bond pairs. Since the lone pair repulsion is greater than bond pair repulsion, the two bond pairs deviate from the lone pairs and come near to each other at 104.5°. This makes a distortion in the tetrahedral geometry and develops an angular shape.

           ●●        ●●   ← two lone pairs squeeze from above
             ╲      ╱
                A
              ╱   ╲
            ╱ 104.5° ╲
          X           X
Angular / bent — two bond pairs + two lone pairs
📐
Watch the angle shrink

109.5° (CH4, no lone pairs) → 107° (NH3, one lone pair) → 104.5° (H2O, two lone pairs). All three start as a tetrahedron of four electron pairs; each added lone pair squeezes the bond angle a little further.

The 3-step method for any molecule
  1. Draw the Lewis structure and count bond pairs and lone pairs on the central atom (ignore π electrons).
  2. Add them to get the total active electron pairs.
  3. Match the count against Table 3.2 and read off the shape.

🧮 Example 3.1 — Worked in Full

Example 3.1 Predict the shape of CH4, NH3, H2O and CO2 molecules on the basis of VSEPR.
CH4 — molecule

We begin by drawing a Lewis structure to determine the number of electron pairs around the central atom.

            H
            ××
      H ×× C ×× H
            ××
            H

No. of Bond pair electrons = 4 · No. of Lone pair electrons = 0

According to VSEPR, these four electron pairs repel each other and are directed towards the corners of a tetrahedron at an angle of 109.5°, hence the geometry of the molecule is tetrahedral.

NH3 — molecule
            ●●
      H ×× N ×× H
            ××
            H

About the central atom: Bond pairs of electrons = 3 · Lone pairs of electrons = 1

According to VSEPR, the repulsion of the lone pair is stronger, which reduces the bond angle from 109.5° to 107°. Hence the predicted shape of the NH3 molecule is trigonal pyramidal.

H2O — molecule
            ●●
      H ×× O ●●
            ××
            H

Electronic information around the central atom: No. of Bond pair electrons = 2 · No. of Lone pair electrons = 2

The repulsion of the two lone pair electrons reduces the bond angle from 109.5° to 104.5°. Hence the shape of the H2O molecule is angular.

CO2 — molecule
      O ×× C ×× O          O ══ C ══ O
                                180°

This shows that two sigma and two pi bonds exist in the molecule. According to VSEPR theory, pi bonds are not involved in the repulsion and geometry, because these are inactive electron pairs. Since only two active electron pairs are found around the carbon atom, its shape is Linear.

💡
Do You Know? — more than one central atom

In many molecules there is more than one central atom. In these molecules we analyse one central atom at a time to determine the active sets of electrons and to predict the geometry. For example, in the ethene molecule we first consider the left-hand carbon atom to analyse the geometry, then carry out a similar analysis for the right-hand carbon atom.

Self Assessment According to the VSEPR model, what molecular shape would you predict for (i) BF3  (ii) CS2  (iii) CBr4?
(i) BF3

Boron is the central atom with 3 bond pairs and 0 lone pairs → type AX3, 3 active electron pairs → Trigonal planar at 120°.

(ii) CS2

Carbon is the central atom, joined to each sulphur by a double bond (S=C=S). The π electrons are not counted, so there are 2 active electron pairs and 0 lone pairs → type AX2 → Linear at 180°.

(iii) CBr4

Carbon is the central atom with 4 bond pairs and 0 lone pairs → type AX4 → Tetrahedral at 109.5°.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Balloons tied together 🎈 — Tie two balloons at their necks and they point opposite ways (180°). Three spread into a triangle (120°). Four form a tetrahedron (109.5°). Electron pairs do exactly the same thing, for exactly the same reason.
  2. The pushy passenger 🚌 — A lone pair is a passenger who takes up more room than everyone else, so all the others get squashed together. That is why NH3 is 107° and H2O is 104.5° instead of a tidy 109.5°.
  3. Same skeleton, different look 💀 — CH4, NH3 and H2O all have four electron pairs, so all three start as a tetrahedron. What changes the visible shape is how many of those corners are invisible lone pairs.
  4. Ignoring the passengers in the boot 🧳 — π electrons are along for the ride but do not affect the shape, so VSEPR ignores them. That is the only reason CO2, with four bonds' worth of electrons, still counts as just two pairs.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "A double bond counts as two electron pairs."

❌ In VSEPR a multiple bond is treated as a single bond, because π bonds are inactive electron pairs and do not alter the idealized geometry. CO2 has two C=O double bonds but only two active electron pairs — hence linear.

⚠️ "Lone pairs don't matter because you can't see them."

❌ Lone pairs are counted as an active set of electrons and they repel more strongly than bond pairs. They are precisely what turns a tetrahedron into a pyramid (NH3) or a bent shape (H2O).

⚠️ "H2O and CO2 should have the same shape — both are AX2."

❌ CO2 is AX2 (2 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs) → linear. H2O is AX2E2 (2 bond pairs, 2 lone pairs) → angular. The lone pairs are the entire difference.

⚠️ "Bond pair–bond pair repulsion is the strongest."

❌ It is the weakest. The order is lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp. Getting this backwards makes every bond-angle question come out wrong.

⚠️ "Trigonal planar and trigonal pyramidal are the same thing."

❌ Trigonal planar = AX3, 3 bond pairs, flat, 120° (BF3). Trigonal pyramidal = AX3E, 3 bond pairs plus a lone pair, cone-shaped, 107° (NH3).

Short Questions

Q6. Differentiate between bond pairs and lone pairs.

Answer: Bond pairs result from the sharing of unpaired electrons of the central atom with unpaired electrons of surrounding atoms. Lone pairs are the paired electrons of the central atom which do not take part in sharing (also called non-bonding pairs). Both are counted as an active set of electrons in VSEPR, but lone pairs repel more strongly.

Q7. Why is the bond angle of H2O (104.5°) smaller than that of NH3 (107°)?

Answer: Both start from a tetrahedral arrangement of four electron pairs (109.5°). NH3 has one lone pair, which squeezes the bond angle down to 107°. H2O has two lone pairs, and since lone pair–lone pair repulsion is the strongest, the two bond pairs are pushed even closer together, to 104.5°.

Q8. Predict the shapes of AlCl3, CCl4, PH3 and H2S.

Answer:
  • AlCl3 — AX3, 3 bp, 0 lp → trigonal planar, 120°
  • CCl4 — AX4, 4 bp, 0 lp → tetrahedral, 109.5°
  • PH3 — AX3E, 3 bp, 1 lp → trigonal pyramidal, 107°
  • H2S — AX2E2, 2 bp, 2 lp → angular (bent), 104.5°

Q9. Write the six postulates of VSEPR theory.

Answer:
  1. Two types of electron pairs surround the central atom — bond pairs and lone pairs, both active sets of electrons.
  2. Being similarly charged (negative), bond pairs and lone pairs repel each other.
  3. Due to repulsion the electron pairs try to be as far apart as possible, minimizing the force of repulsion.
  4. Repulsion order: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp.
  5. For double and triple bonds, π electron pairs are not active and are not included in the count.
  6. The shape depends on the total number of electron pairs (bonding and lone).

📝 Summary

5 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. VSEPR (Sidgwick and Powell, 1940): shape is decided by the repulsion between electron pairs in the valence shell of the central atom, which arrange themselves as far apart as possible.
  2. Two kinds of pairs — bond pairs (shared) and lone pairs (unshared) — and both count. Repulsion order: lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp.
  3. π electrons are inactive and are never counted; a multiple bond is treated as a single bond.
  4. The five shapes: AX2 linear 180° · AX3 trigonal planar 120° · AX4 tetrahedral 109.5° · AX3E trigonal pyramidal 107° · AX2E2 angular 104.5°.
  5. Method: draw the Lewis structure → count bond pairs and lone pairs on the central atom → match against Table 3.2.

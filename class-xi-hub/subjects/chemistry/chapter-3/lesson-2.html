Molecular Orbital Theory

Chapter 3 · Lesson 2 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 14–16 min read Topic 3.1.2

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Hold a magnet near liquid oxygen and it sticks. Liquid nitrogen does nothing. Oxygen is magnetic — and Valence Bond Theory cannot explain why.

VBT says O2 has a neat double bond with all electrons paired up. Paired electrons are not attracted to magnets. Yet the experiment is unambiguous: O2 is paramagnetic, so it must have unpaired electrons somewhere.

Molecular Orbital Theory finds them. It also gives you something VBT never could — a number called bond order that tells you instantly whether a bond is single, double or triple, or whether the molecule can exist at all.

📖 Core Concept: Orbitals That Belong to the Whole Molecule

📚 Who and when

Mullikan, Hund and Huckel (1927) developed an alternate theory known as molecular orbital theory to overcome the shortcomings of VBT.

The five assumptions of MOT

📚 Assumption (i)

Atomic orbitals of similar energies linearly combine together to produce molecular orbitals.

📚 Assumption (ii)

Combination of two atomic orbitals (A.O) gives two molecular orbitals (MO) — one of them has lower energy and the other has higher energy than any of the atomic orbitals from which they formed.

📚 Assumption (iii)

The molecular orbital with lower energy is called the bonding molecular orbital (BMO), and the orbital with higher energy is called the anti bonding molecular orbital (ABMO).

📚 Assumption (iv)

The movement of electrons in a M.O is influenced by both nuclei of the combining atoms.

📚 Assumption (v)

The filling of electrons into the molecular orbital follows the fundamental rules of electronic configuration, i.e. the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule and Pauli rule.

🔑
The one difference that matters

In VBT each atom keeps its own orbital and just shares a pair. In MOT the atomic orbitals lose their individual identity and merge into new orbitals that belong to the whole molecule. Every other difference between the two theories follows from this.

⚖️ Characteristics of Bonding and Anti Bonding M.O

📚 Bonding molecular orbital (BMO)

The bonding molecular orbital has lower energy than the atomic orbitals from which it is formed. Electrons in bonding molecular orbitals favour bonding, because the combining orbitals interact constructively — thus the probability of electron density is maximum between the two nuclei.

📚 Anti bonding molecular orbital (ABMO)

The anti bonding molecular orbital has higher energy than the two combining atomic orbitals. It is produced due to destructive interaction, and hence the electron density in the region between the two nuclei is zero, since a nodal plane bisects the inter-nucleus axis.

                        ┌─────────────┐
                        │  σ*  ABMO   │   higher energy
                        └─────────────┘   destructive · nodal plane
                          ╱         ╲     electron density = 0 between nuclei
                        ╱             ╲
      ┌──────┐                          ┌──────┐
      │ A.O  │                          │ A.O  │
      └──────┘                          └──────┘
                        ╲             ╱
                          ╲         ╱
                        ┌─────────────┐
                        │  σ   BMO    │   lower energy
                        └─────────────┘   constructive · density max
                                          between the two nuclei
Two atomic orbitals always give one BMO and one ABMO

🔢 Bond Order

Molecular orbital theory (MOT) is best to predict the bond order and the magnetic properties of molecules. The formula used in determining the bond order is:

Bond order = N(b) − N(a)2

Where:

  • N(b) = Electrons in bonding M.O
  • N(a) = Electrons in anti bonding M.O
💡
Reading a bond order

1 = single bond · 2 = double bond · 3 = triple bond. A bond order of 0 means the molecule cannot exist. And the higher the bond order, the shorter and stronger the bond — a fact you will use again in Lesson 5.

🧲
Do You Know? — para vs dia

Substances that have unpaired electrons in their orbitals are attracted by an external magnetic field and are said to be paramagnetic — for example the O2 molecule. Substances that have paired electrons in their orbitals are repelled by an external magnetic field and are said to be diamagnetic — for example H2, N2, He etc.

🅗 The H2 Molecule

A hydrogen atom contains one electron in the 1s orbital. The atomic orbitals of both hydrogen atoms linearly combine to form two molecular orbitals. According to the rule of electronic configuration, electrons accommodate in the bonding MO since it possesses low energy, hence the anti bonding MO remains vacant.

                     ┌──────────────┐
                     │ Antibonding  │   (vacant)
                     │     M.O      │
                     └──────────────┘
                       ╱          ╲
     A.O  [ ↑ ]                        [ ↑ ]  A.O
      H                                        H
                       ╲          ╱
                     ┌──────────────┐
                     │   [ ↑↓ ]     │
                     │  Bonding M.O │
                     └──────────────┘
Fig 3.2 — molecular orbital energy diagram of H2
Worked Bond order of the H2 molecule
Step 1 — count the electrons

N(b) = 2 (both electrons sit in the bonding M.O) · N(a) = 0 (anti bonding M.O is vacant).

Step 2 — apply the formula
Bond order of H2 = N(b) − N(a)2 = 2 − 02 = 1
Step 3 — magnetic behaviour

Since there is no unpaired electron in any molecular orbital, hydrogen is diamagnetic in nature.

🅞 The O2 Molecule — and Why It Is Magnetic

The electronic configuration of oxygen (z = 8) is 1s2, 2s2, 2p4. Thus there are five atomic orbitals with eight electrons in each oxygen atom. These five atomic orbitals of both oxygen atoms combine to form ten molecular orbitals.

Molecular orbital configuration

The molecular orbital configuration with increasing energy order is given as:

σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < π2py = π2pz < σ2px < π*2py = π*2pz < σ*2px

Filling the 16 electrons of O2 into these orbitals:

   σ1s²  σ*1s²  σ2s²  σ*2s²  σ2px²  π2py²  π2pz²  π*2py¹  π*2pz¹
   ───   ────   ───   ────   ─────  ─────  ─────  ──────  ──────
    2  +  2  +   2  +   2  +   2  +   2  +   2  +   1   +   1   = 16 e⁻

   BONDING  N(b) = σ1s² + σ2s² + σ2px² + π2py² + π2pz²  = 10
   ANTI     N(a) = σ*1s² + σ*2s² + π*2py¹ + π*2pz¹      =  6

   ↑        ↑
   π*2py    π*2pz     ← TWO UNPAIRED ELECTRONS (Hund's rule)
Fig 3.3 — molecular orbital energy diagram of O2
Worked Bond order and magnetic behaviour of O2
Step 1 — count

N(b) = 10  ·  N(a) = 6

Step 2 — apply the formula
Bond order of O2 = N(b) − N(a)2 = 10 − 62 = 2

A bond order of 2 confirms the double bond in O=O.

Step 3 — magnetic behaviour

Since there are two unpaired electrons in the anti bonding MO (π*2py and π*2pz, one each by Hund's rule), O2 is paramagnetic.

🏆
This is MOT's headline achievement

VBT draws O2 with every electron neatly paired and therefore predicts it should be diamagnetic — which is wrong. MOT puts two electrons singly into the two π* orbitals and predicts paramagnetic — which matches the experiment. Same molecule, same double bond, but only one theory gets the magnetism right.

📌
A note on the printed energy order

The textbook's written sequence puts π2py = π2pz below σ2px, while Fig 3.3 draws σ2px below the π orbitals — which is the order most books use for O2 and F2. Either way, the bond order is still 2 and O2 is still paramagnetic, because the same orbitals hold the same electrons. Write the sequence as your textbook prints it in the exam.

Self Assessment Write the molecular orbital energy diagram of the N2 molecule and determine its bond order. Also predict whether it is paramagnetic or diamagnetic.
Step 1 — count the electrons

Nitrogen has z = 7, so each atom has 7 electrons. The N2 molecule has 14 electrons to place.

Step 2 — fill the molecular orbitals
   σ*2px  ┌────┐                     (vacant)
   π*2py  ┌────┐ π*2pz ┌────┐        (vacant)
   σ2px   ┌ ↑↓ ┐

   σ1s    ┌ ↑↓ ┐
σ1s² σ*1s² σ2s² σ*2s² π2py² π2pz² σ2px²
Step 3 — count bonding and anti bonding electrons

N(b) = σ1s² + σ2s² + π2py² + π2pz² + σ2px² = 10

N(a) = σ*1s² + σ*2s² = 4

Step 4 — bond order
Bond order of N2 = 10 − 42 = 62 = 3

This confirms the triple bond in N≡N.

Step 5 — magnetic behaviour

Every molecular orbital is either completely filled or completely empty, so there are no unpaired electrons. Therefore N2 is diamagnetic.

📊 Valence Bond Theory vs Molecular Orbital Theory

This four-row comparison appears directly in the chapter exercise. Learn it.

Valence Bond TheoryMolecular Orbital Theory
Tells that only some valence electrons are involved in bond formation. Tells that bond formation occurs due to the involvement of all the valence electrons of interacting atoms.
Both the concerned atomic orbitals possess their individual identity. Both the concerned atomic orbitals do not possess their individual identity.
VBT doesn't introduce any adequate idea about the bond order. MOT gives an adequate idea about the bond order, by virtue of which the bond can be identified as single, double or triple.
The paramagnetic behaviour of molecules such as O2 cannot be explained. The paramagnetic behaviour of molecules is very well explained.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Two flats vs one shared house 🏠 — VBT is two neighbours who keep their own flats and just share a garden. MOT knocks both flats down and builds one house that both families live in. That is why the atomic orbitals lose their identity.
  2. Two waves meeting 🌊 — Add two waves in phase and they reinforce into a big one (bonding MO). Add them out of phase and they cancel to nothing (anti bonding MO, with zero density at the nodal plane). Same water, two outcomes.
  3. Tug of war 🪢 — Bonding electrons pull the atoms together; anti bonding electrons push them apart. Bond order is just "pullers minus pushers, divided by two". If they tie, bond order is 0 and the molecule falls apart.
  4. The magnet test 🧲 — Pour liquid oxygen between magnet poles and it hangs there. Pour liquid nitrogen and it falls straight through. Two unpaired electrons versus none — exactly what the MO diagrams predict.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Anti bonding orbitals are always empty."

❌ They are empty in H2, but in O2 the anti bonding orbitals hold six electrons — and the two unpaired ones there are exactly what makes oxygen paramagnetic. Always count them.

⚠️ "Bond order = number of bonding electrons."

❌ Bond order = [N(b) − N(a)] ÷ 2. You must subtract the anti bonding electrons and then halve the result. Forgetting the ÷2 is the single most common numerical slip in this topic.

⚠️ "Paramagnetic means it is a permanent magnet."

❌ Paramagnetic means it is attracted by an external magnetic field because it has unpaired electrons. Diamagnetic substances (paired electrons) are repelled.

⚠️ "Two atomic orbitals give one molecular orbital."

❌ Two A.O always give two M.O — one bonding (lower energy) and one anti bonding (higher energy). Orbitals are never destroyed, only rearranged.

⚠️ "MOT replaced VBT completely."

⚠️ Both are still used. VBT is better for describing shapes and hybridization (Lessons 3–4); MOT is better for bond order and magnetic properties. The chapter teaches both on purpose.

Numerical & Short Questions

Q6. Differentiate between bonding and anti bonding molecular orbitals.

Answer: A bonding M.O has lower energy than the atomic orbitals that formed it; it results from constructive interaction, so electron density is maximum between the two nuclei and it favours bonding. An anti bonding M.O has higher energy; it results from destructive interaction, so electron density between the nuclei is zero because a nodal plane bisects the inter-nucleus axis.

Q7. Calculate the bond order of a species with N(b) = 8 and N(a) = 4. What kind of bond is it?

Answer: Bond order = (8 − 4)/2 = 4/2 = 2. A bond order of 2 corresponds to a double bond.

Q8. Why can VBT not explain the paramagnetic behaviour of oxygen, while MOT can?

Answer: VBT describes O2 as a double bond in which all electrons are paired, which would make it diamagnetic — contradicting experiment. MOT distributes all the valence electrons into molecular orbitals and shows that the last two electrons occupy the π*2py and π*2pz anti bonding orbitals singly (Hund's rule). These two unpaired electrons make O2 paramagnetic.

Q9. State the five assumptions of Molecular Orbital Theory.

Answer:
  1. Atomic orbitals of similar energies linearly combine to produce molecular orbitals.
  2. Two atomic orbitals give two molecular orbitals — one of lower and one of higher energy than either atomic orbital.
  3. The lower-energy one is the bonding M.O, the higher-energy one is the anti bonding M.O.
  4. Electron movement in a M.O is influenced by both nuclei.
  5. Filling follows the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule and Pauli rule.

📝 Summary

6 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. MOT — Mullikan, Hund and Huckel, 1927 — was developed to overcome the shortcomings of VBT.
  2. Atomic orbitals of similar energy combine; two A.O always give two M.O — a low-energy bonding MO (constructive, density max between nuclei) and a high-energy anti bonding MO (destructive, nodal plane, density zero).
  3. Filling follows the Aufbau principle, Hund's rule and Pauli rule, and electrons are influenced by both nuclei.
  4. Bond order = [N(b) − N(a)] ÷ 2. H2 → 1 (diamagnetic) · O2 → 2 (paramagnetic) · N2 → 3 (diamagnetic).
  5. Unpaired electrons → paramagnetic (attracted, e.g. O2); paired electrons → diamagnetic (repelled, e.g. H2, N2, He).
  6. VBT keeps the atomic orbitals' identity and says nothing useful about bond order or paramagnetism; MOT merges them and explains both.

Valence Bond Theory & the Sigma/Pi Bond

Chapter 3 · Lesson 1 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 14–16 min read Topic 3.1.1

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Last chapter you learned what a single atom looks like inside. The obvious next question is the one this whole chapter answers: why and how do atoms join together to make a molecule?

Here is the short version. A molecule is more stable than the atoms it came from, because it possesses energy lower than the energy of the uncombined atoms. When atoms combine, attractive forces are created which release energy — and the system settles into that lower-energy state.

Lewis told us electrons do the bonding, but he never explained what a bond actually is. Valence Bond Theory does: a bond is two atomic orbitals overlapping. Once you see that, sigma bonds, pi bonds and everything in Chapter 3 follows.

📖 Starting Point: What Is a Chemical Bond?

📚 Textbook Definition

"The attractive force that holds atoms together in a compound is known as the chemical bond."

Studies on chemical bond formation came to materialize during the early 20th century, after the electron had been discovered. Lewis (1916) gave the concept of valency and said that electrons in atoms are responsible for chemical bonding.

The electronic theory of chemical bonding, proposed independently by Kossel and Lewis, tells us that a chemical bond is formed between two atoms to acquire noble gas configuration.

💡
Why noble gases don't bond

Atoms of almost all elements have an incomplete outer shell and tend to complete it by chemical combination — either by sharing or transfer of electrons. Noble gases are extremely stable because their duplet/octet is already complete, so they do not form any type of chemical bond and remain in the atomic state (mono-atomic molecules).

Why we need theories at all

The Lewis concept provides a guideline for writing the structural formula of covalent compounds, but it neither describes the nature of bonding forces, nor the shape of molecules, nor many other properties such as bond energies, bond strength and the paramagnetic behaviour of a molecule.

There are two modern theories which describe the behaviour of electrons in a molecule:

  1. Valence Bond Theory (VBT) — this lesson
  2. Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) — Lesson 2

🔗 Valence Bond Theory (VBT)

📚 Who and when

This theory was introduced by London and Heitler in 1927. It tells how the bonding in a covalent molecule takes place by the overlapping of atomic orbitals.

It not only provides basic information for analysing the structure and the bonding in the molecule, but also the strength of the covalent bond as well as molecular shape, on the basis of atomic orbital interaction.

The five postulates of VBT

📚 Postulate (i)

A covalent bond is formed due to the overlap of half filled orbitals of combining atoms. (The term overlap represents sharing of some common region in space.)

📚 Postulate (ii)

As a result of overlapping, there is a maximum electron cloud somewhere between the two overlapped orbitals.

📚 Postulate (iii)

The electrons present in both overlapped orbitals should be in opposite spin.

📚 Postulate (iv)

The strength of the bond is determined by the extent of overlap. The greater the overlap, the stronger is the bond.

📚 Postulate (v)

Each atom involved in the overlapping keeps its own atomic orbital, but the electron pair is shared up by both atoms which take part in the overlapping.

🔑
Postulate (iv) is the one that keeps paying off

"Greater overlap = stronger bond" explains almost every comparison in this chapter: why sigma beats pi, why p–p beats s–s, and why sp hybrids form stronger bonds than sp3. If you remember one postulate, remember this one.

Valence bond theory describes two ways of overlapping of atomic orbitals, which results in the formation of two types of covalent bonds:

  1. sigma (σ) bond
  2. pi (π) bond

➡️ Sigma (σ) Bond

📚 Definition

This bond is established when the two atomic orbitals overlap linearly, in such a way that both orbitals lie along the line joining the two nuclei. It is defined as "the bond formed by head to head overlapping of half filled atomic orbitals".

The electron density in sigma-bonded atoms is found highest between the two bonded nuclei. The formation of a sigma bond results from the following three types of head to head overlapping.

1. s–s overlapping

It is the overlap of the half filled s orbital of two combining atoms. This type of overlapping exists in the H2 molecule.

A hydrogen atom contains just one electron in the 1s-orbital. When two hydrogen atoms come closer to each other, their half filled s-orbitals overlap and form an H–H sigma bond in which the electron cloud is rich along the axis between the two nuclei.

     (H↑)    +    (H↓)    ──────→    (H↑↓H)
    H atom       H atom            H₂ molecule

    1st H atom   [ ↑ ]
                          ←── σ bond
    2nd H atom   [ ↓ ]
s–s overlapping in H2

2. s–p overlapping

It is the overlap of a half filled s and a half filled p orbital of two combining atoms. Examples of molecules in which the sigma bond is formed due to s–p overlapping are HF, HCl etc.

The HF molecule is formed by the overlap of the half filled 1s orbital of hydrogen and the half filled 2pz orbital of fluorine.

   H atom                [ ↑ ]
                            ┊         ←── σ bond
   F atom  [1↓] [1↓]  [1↓][1↓][ ↓ ]
            1s    2s    2px  2py  2pz
s–p overlapping in HF

3. p–p overlapping

It is the overlap of the half filled p orbital of two combining atoms. The example of this type of overlapping is the F2 molecule.

The electronic configuration of fluorine is 1s2 2s2 2px2 2py2 2pz1 — it means only the pz orbital is occupied by a single electron and available for overlap. VBT tells that the half filled pz orbitals of both fluorine atoms undergo overlapping, in which the electron pair is shared up between the two atoms.

            1s     2s      2px  2py  2pz
   F atom  [1↓]  [1↓]    [1↓][1↓][ ↑ ]
                                    ┊    ←── σ bond
   F atom  [1↓]  [1↓]    [1↓][1↓][ ↓ ]
p–p overlapping in F2

⤴️ Pi (π) Bond

📚 Definition

This bond is formed by the lateral or side-way overlap of p-orbitals between two atoms which are already bonded through a sigma bond. The condition for the formation of a pi bond is that the two p-orbitals should be parallel to each other.

  • The electron cloud of a pi bond is unsymmetrical and is concentrated above and below the plane of the sigma bond.
  • In this situation the rotation of the p-orbital becomes restricted between the two atoms.
  • Since the region of overlapping of p-orbitals in the formation of a pi bond is small (minimum), it is relatively weaker than a sigma bond.

Examples of molecules which possess a pi bond are O2 and N2 etc.

How O2 gets one sigma and one pi bond

The electronic configuration of oxygen is 1s2, 2s2, 2px2, 2py1, 2pz1. Since there are two half filled p orbitals on each oxygen atom:

  • One of them undergoes head to head overlapping → forms the sigma bond.
  • The overlapping of the other p-orbital takes place laterally → leads to the formation of the pi bond.
📌
The order is fixed, never the other way round

The first bond between two atoms is always a sigma bond. A pi bond can only form after a sigma bond already exists. So a double bond = 1σ + 1π, and a triple bond = 1σ + 2π.

💪 Strength of Bond in Terms of Valence Bond Theory

An important feature of valence bond theory is that the strength of a covalent bond is proportional to the extent of overlapping of atomic orbitals. The greater the overlapping, the stronger the bond between the atoms, and the higher the energy required to break the bond.

Why s–s is the weakest sigma bond

Due to spherical charge distribution in the s-orbital, generally s–s overlap is not so effective as s–p and p–p overlap. Since p-orbitals have directional charge distribution and longer lobes, they cause more effective overlap. Thus the s–s sigma bond is relatively weaker than s–p and p–p sigma bonds.

Sigma bond strength:   s–s  <  s–p  <  p–p

Why pi is weaker than sigma

In a pi (π) bond the orbital has two regions of electronic density — one above the nuclear axis and the other below the nuclear axis. This type of overlapping of atomic orbitals is not maximum; that is why a pi bond is weaker than a sigma bond.

   SIGMA — head to head            PI — side by side
   (big overlap, strong)           (small overlap, weak)

                                       ∪∪∪∪
   electron cloud dense           electron cloud above
   ON the bond axis               and BELOW the axis
Extent of overlap decides bond strength

This comparison is a standard exam question. Learn all four rows.

Sigma BondPi Bond
It is formed by head to head overlapping of half filled atomic orbitals. It is formed by lateral/parallel overlapping of half filled atomic orbitals.

❌ Limitations of Valence Bond Theory

Although VBT furnishes a good picture of bonding in molecules, it has some shortcomings:

  • It fails to describe the valencies in Carbon, Boron and Beryllium.
  • It does not tell about the paramagnetic behaviour of oxygen.
  • It fails to describe the delocalization of electrons in the molecule.
🔗
Each limitation gets its own fix later

The carbon/boron/beryllium valency problem is solved by hybridization (Lesson 4). The oxygen paramagnetism problem is solved by Molecular Orbital Theory (Lesson 2). This is why the chapter is ordered the way it is.

🧮 Solved Self-Assessment (from the book)

Self Assessment (i) Why is a sigma bond stronger than a pi bond?
Answer

Because the strength of a bond depends on the extent of overlapping (VBT postulate iv).

A sigma bond is formed by head to head overlapping, so the orbitals meet along the line joining the nuclei and the overlap is maximum. The electron cloud is densest directly between the two nuclei.

A pi bond is formed by side-way (lateral) overlapping of parallel p-orbitals. The region of overlap is small (minimum) and the electron cloud is split above and below the bond axis rather than concentrated between the nuclei.

Greater overlap ⟹ stronger bond. Hence sigma > pi.

Self Assessment (ii) Why is a p–p sigma bond stronger than s–s and s–p?
Answer

Because of the shape of the orbitals involved.

An s-orbital has spherical charge distribution, so its charge is spread evenly in all directions. It cannot concentrate its electron density along one direction, so s–s overlap is not very effective.

A p-orbital has directional charge distribution and longer lobes, so it can point straight at its partner and produce a more effective overlap.

Therefore the extent of overlap increases in the order s–s < s–p < p–p, and so does the bond strength.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Shaking hands 🤝 — A sigma bond is a proper handshake: palms meet head-on, maximum contact, firm grip. A pi bond is brushing fingertips sideways — contact exists, but it is weak and easily broken.
  2. Ball vs torch beam 🔦 — An s-orbital is a glowing ball, spreading light everywhere. A p-orbital is a torch beam pointed one way. Two torches aimed at each other overlap far better than two glowing balls — that is why p–p beats s–s.
  3. A door hinge vs a nailed plank 🚪 — With only a sigma bond, the two atoms can rotate freely, like a door on a hinge. Add a pi bond and rotation locks, like nailing a second plank across it.
  4. Rolling downhill ⛰️ — Atoms bond because the molecule sits at lower energy than the separate atoms. Things fall to the lowest place they can reach; the energy released on the way down is what holds the bond together.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Any two orbitals can overlap to make a bond."

❌ VBT requires half filled orbitals whose electrons have opposite spin. A fully filled orbital has no room, and same-spin electrons cannot pair up.

⚠️ "A double bond is just two sigma bonds."

❌ A double bond is one sigma + one pi. A triple bond is one sigma + two pi. Only one sigma bond can ever exist between two atoms, and at most two pi bonds.

⚠️ "Pi bonds form first because they look stronger in diagrams."

❌ The sigma bond always forms first. A pi bond only forms between atoms that are already joined by a sigma bond — that is stated in the definition.

⚠️ "Atoms lose their orbitals when they bond."

❌ Postulate (v): each atom keeps its own atomic orbital; only the electron pair is shared. (This is exactly the point where MOT disagrees with VBT — see Lesson 2.)

⚠️ "VBT explains everything about bonding."

❌ It fails on three things: the valencies of C, B and Be, the paramagnetic behaviour of oxygen, and delocalization of electrons.

Short Questions

Q6. Define a chemical bond and state why noble gases do not form bonds.

Answer: "The attractive force that holds atoms together in a compound is known as the chemical bond." Noble gases are extremely stable due to the completion of their duplet/octet, so they have no incomplete outer shell to fill. They therefore do not form any type of chemical bond and remain in the atomic state (mono-atomic molecules).

Q7. State the five postulates of Valence Bond Theory.

Answer:
  1. A covalent bond forms due to the overlap of half filled orbitals of the combining atoms.
  2. Overlapping produces a maximum electron cloud between the two overlapped orbitals.
  3. The electrons in both overlapped orbitals must be of opposite spin.
  4. Bond strength is determined by the extent of overlap — greater overlap, stronger bond.
  5. Each atom keeps its own atomic orbital, but the electron pair is shared by both atoms.

Q8. Why can a molecule with only a sigma bond rotate freely, while one with a pi bond cannot?

Answer: A sigma bond is formed along the line joining the two nuclei, so rotating about that axis does not disturb the overlap — free rotation is possible. A pi bond needs the two p-orbitals to stay parallel to each other; rotating would break that alignment and destroy the overlap. Hence the rotation of the p-orbital becomes restricted when a pi bond is present.

Q9. How many sigma and pi bonds are present in O2? Explain using its configuration.

Answer: One sigma and one pi bond. The configuration of oxygen is 1s2, 2s2, 2px2, 2py1, 2pz1, so each oxygen atom has two half filled p orbitals. One pair overlaps head to head → sigma bond; the other pair overlaps laterally → pi bond. Together they make the O=O double bond.

📝 Summary

6 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. A chemical bond is the attractive force holding atoms together. Atoms bond to reach noble gas configuration (Kossel and Lewis); a molecule is stable because its energy is lower than that of the free atoms.
  2. VBT (London and Heitler, 1927): a covalent bond is the overlap of half filled orbitals with electrons of opposite spin; greater overlap = stronger bond; each atom keeps its own orbital.
  3. Sigma bond = head to head overlap, formed by s–s (H2), s–p (HF, HCl) and p–p (F2) overlapping.
  4. Pi bond = lateral/side-way overlap of parallel p-orbitals, only between atoms already joined by a sigma bond; cloud sits above and below the axis; weaker than sigma. Examples: O2, N2.
  5. Strength order: s–s < s–p < p–p (p-orbitals are directional with longer lobes) and pi < sigma (smaller overlap).
  6. VBT fails on the valencies of C, B, Be, the paramagnetism of O2, and electron delocalization.

Stoichiometric Calculations

Chapter 1 · Lesson 3 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 14–16 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

A chef follows a recipe: "2 cups of flour make 1 cake." From that, they know 6 cups make 3 cakes. A balanced chemical equation is a recipe — it tells you exactly how much product you get from a given amount of reactant.

In this lesson we use that recipe to calculate unknown masses and volumes: how much CO2 burning fuel makes, how much oxygen a reaction releases, and so on.

📖 Core Concept: The Mole-Ratio Method

The numbers in front of the formulas in a balanced equation (the coefficients) give the mole ratio — the recipe. Every stoichiometry problem follows the same four steps:

  1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
  2. Convert the known quantity into moles (mass ÷ molar mass, or volume ÷ 22.4 for a gas at STP).
  3. Use the mole ratio from the equation to find moles of the unknown.
  4. Convert moles of the unknown back into the required mass or volume.
🧭
Three flavours, one method

Mass–mass: given a mass, find a mass.  Mass–volume: given a mass, find a gas volume.  Volume–volume: given a gas volume, find a gas volume. All three use the exact same four steps.

💡 Worked Examples

A · Mass → Mass

Example 1.7 Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced by the complete thermal decomposition of 50 g of limestone (CaCO3).
Step 1 — balanced equation
CaCO3 heat→ CaO + CO2
Step 2 — known → moles (molar mass CaCO3 = 100 g/mol)
Moles of CaCO3 = 50100 = 0.5 mole
Step 3 — mole ratio

From the equation, 1 mole of CaCO3 gives 1 mole of CO2. So 0.5 mole CaCO3 → 0.5 mole CO2.

Step 4 — moles → mass (molar mass CO2 = 44 g/mol)
Mass of CO2 = 0.5 × 44
Answer= 22 g of CO2

B · Mass → Volume of Gas

Example 1.8 49 g of potassium chlorate (KClO3) decomposes completely. Determine the volume of oxygen gas liberated at STP.
Step 1 — balanced equation
2KClO3 heat→ 2KCl + 3O2
Step 2 — known → moles (molar mass KClO3 = 122.5 g/mol)
Moles of KClO3 = 49122.5 = 0.4 mole
Step 3 — mole ratio

2 moles KClO3 give 3 moles O2, so:

Moles of O2 = 32 × 0.4 = 0.6 mole
Step 4 — moles → volume (× 22.4 at STP)
Volume of O2 = 0.6 × 22.4
Answer= 13.44 dm3 of O2 at STP

C · Volume → Volume of Gas

Example 1.9 Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide at STP produced by the complete burning of 50 dm3 of butane (C4H10) in excess oxygen.
Step 1 — balanced equation
2C4H10 + 13O2 heat→ 8CO2 + 10H2O
Shortcut for gases

For gases at the same temperature and pressure, the mole ratio equals the volume ratio (Avogadro's law). So we can use the coefficients directly as volumes:

2 dm3 butane → 8 dm3 CO2
50 dm3 butane → 82 × 50
Answer= 200 dm3 of CO2 at STP
⚡
Volume–volume is fastest

When both the known and unknown are gases at the same conditions, skip moles entirely — just use the coefficient ratio as a volume ratio. This only works gas-to-gas.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "I can use the ratio with an unbalanced equation."

❌ Never. The mole ratio is only correct once the equation is balanced. Balance first, always — otherwise every later step is wrong.

⚠️ "I can take the volume ratio of a solid and a gas."

❌ The volume-ratio shortcut works only between gases. A solid like CaCO3 has no "22.4 dm³" — for solids you must use mass and moles.

⚠️ "Mole ratio means 1 : 1 always."

✅ Only if the coefficients are equal. In 2KClO3 → 2KCl + 3O2 the ratio of KClO3 to O2 is 2 : 3, not 1 : 1. Read the coefficients carefully.

Short Questions

Try the full four steps before revealing.

Q4. What mass of CaO is formed when 100 g of CaCO3 decomposes? (CaCO3 = 100, CaO = 56)

Answer: Moles of CaCO3 = 100 ÷ 100 = 1 mole → 1 mole CaO → mass = 1 × 56 = 56 g of CaO.

Q5. What volume of O2 at STP is released from 245 g of KClO3? (KClO3 = 122.5)

Answer: Moles KClO3 = 245 ÷ 122.5 = 2 moles → O2 = (3/2) × 2 = 3 moles → volume = 3 × 22.4 = 67.2 dm3.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. A balanced equation is a recipe; its coefficients give the mole ratio.
  2. Four steps every time: balance → known to moles → mole ratio → unknown to mass/volume.
  3. For gas-to-gas at the same conditions, the volume ratio equals the coefficient ratio — no moles needed.

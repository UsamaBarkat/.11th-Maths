Elements, Compounds, and Mixtures

Chapter 1 · Lesson 3 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 12–15 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Look at your kitchen right now. There is salt on the shelf — two completely different substances (sodium and chlorine) locked together. There is air all around you — a loose blend of gases that you can separate any time. And the water you drink is a compound with its own fixed formula.

Everything in the universe is either an element, a compound, or a mixture. Once you know the difference, you can look at any substance — lassi, brass, sea water, rust — and instantly say what type it is. That is exactly what this lesson teaches you.

1. Elements

📚 Textbook Definition

An element is a pure substance that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any chemical or physical method. It is made of only one kind of atom.

✅ Easy Version

An element is the simplest form of matter. No matter how much you heat it, burn it, or react it — you will never get something simpler. Gold is always gold. Oxygen is always oxygen.

There are 118 known elements, all listed in the Periodic Table. They are divided into three broad groups:

2. Compounds

📚 Textbook Definition

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more elements combine chemically in a fixed ratio. The properties of a compound are completely different from the elements that form it. It can only be broken down by chemical means.

✅ Easy Version

A compound is when elements join together so tightly — through a chemical bond — that they lose their own identity and become something totally new. Salt (NaCl) is made of sodium (a dangerous metal) and chlorine (a poisonous gas) — but together they make something safe to eat!

CompoundFormulaElements insideReal-life use

⭐ Exam point: In a compound, the ratio is always fixed. Water is always H₂O — never H₃O or HO. This fixed ratio is what makes a compound a pure substance.

3. Mixtures

📚 Textbook Definition

A mixture is a combination of two or more substances (elements or compounds) that are not chemically combined. Each substance keeps its own properties, the composition can vary, and the components can be separated by physical methods.

✅ Easy Version

A mixture is when you just put things together without any chemical reaction happening. Like daal and rice on your plate — they sit together but are not joined. You can pick them apart with a spoon.

6. How to Separate Mixtures

Because the parts of a mixture are not chemically joined, physical methods can separate them:

TechniqueHow it worksReal-life example
FiltrationPasses mixture through filter paper — solid stays, liquid passesRemoving mud from dirty water
EvaporationHeating drives off the liquid, leaving the dissolved solidGetting salt back from salt water
DistillationBoiling a liquid, collecting and cooling the vaporGetting pure water from sea water; separating petrol from crude oil
Magnetic SeparationA magnet pulls out magnetic metalsSeparating iron filings from sand
ChromatographyComponents travel at different speeds through a mediumSeparating ink dyes; testing food colors in lab

💡 Real-Life Examples

  1. Table salt 🧂 — NaCl is a compound. Sodium alone violently explodes in water. Chlorine alone is a deadly gas. But together, chemically bonded in a 1:1 ratio, they form the safe salt you put on your food every day.
  2. Lassi 🥛 — Lassi (yogurt + water + sugar) is a heterogeneous or homogeneous mixture depending on how well it is blended. You can separate the fat by churning — no chemical reaction needed.
  3. Sea water 🌊 — Sea water is a homogeneous mixture of water, salt, and dissolved gases. Evaporate it and you get salt back — proof it is a mixture, not a compound.
  4. Brass utensils 🫕 — Your brass degchi is a homogeneous mixture (alloy) of copper and zinc. You can vary the amounts — not all brass is identical. That variable composition is the mark of a mixture.
  5. Air 🌬️ — Air is a homogeneous mixture of roughly 78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and small amounts of CO₂ and other gases. Hospitals use fractional distillation to separate pure oxygen from air.
  6. Sand from the beach 🏖️ — Beach sand is a heterogeneous mixture of silicon dioxide, shell fragments, and minerals. You can see the different particles — classic heterogeneous mixture.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ Confusion #1: "Air is a compound because it has many gases"

❌ No! In air, gases are just mixed — not chemically joined. The ratio of gases in air can vary from place to place (more CO₂ in a city, more O₂ on a mountain). Compounds always have a fixed ratio. So air is a mixture.

⚠️ Confusion #2: "Salt water is a compound"

❌ No! Just boil or evaporate salt water — you get the salt back without any chemical reaction. No chemical reaction needed means it is a mixture (homogeneous). In a true compound you need a chemical reaction to break it apart.

⚠️ Confusion #3: "Compounds and mixtures are the same thing"

❌ Big difference! In a compound, elements lose their identity — sodium and chlorine no longer exist separately in salt. In a mixture, every part keeps its own identity — you can taste the salt and feel the water in salt water separately.

🔑 Quick Memory Trick

Element = one type only (Everything pure, one kind)
Compound = Chemically combined, fixed ratio (Completely new substance)
Mixture = Manually mixed, separable (Mix and unmix freely)

Q5. Define element and compound. Give two examples of each.

Answer:
An element is a pure substance made of only one type of atom that cannot be broken down by any chemical method. Examples: Iron (Fe), Oxygen (O).

A compound is a pure substance formed when two or more elements chemically combine in a fixed ratio, producing a substance with entirely new properties. Examples: Water (H₂O), Salt (NaCl).

Q6. What is the difference between a compound and a mixture? Give one example of each.

Answer:
In a compound, elements are chemically combined in a fixed ratio. The components lose their original properties, and separation requires a chemical reaction. Example: Water (H₂O).

In a mixture, substances are physically combined in any ratio. Each component keeps its own properties, and separation is done by physical methods. Example: Salt water (salt + water — you can evaporate to separate).

Q7. Classify the following as element, compound, or mixture: (a) Gold  (b) CO₂  (c) Muddy water  (d) Brass

Answer:
(a) Gold → Element (made of only gold atoms; cannot be broken down further)
(b) CO₂ → Compound (carbon and oxygen chemically combined in fixed ratio 1:2)
(c) Muddy water → Heterogeneous mixture (mud and water mixed, not chemically joined; visible separate parts)
(d) Brass → Homogeneous mixture / alloy (copper and zinc mixed; composition can vary; looks uniform)

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Elements are the simplest pure substances (one type of atom, 118 known, cannot be broken down chemically). Compounds form when two or more elements chemically combine in a fixed ratio — the result has completely new properties different from its elements.
  2. Mixtures have a variable composition, each component keeps its own properties, and they are separated by physical methods (filtration, evaporation, distillation, magnetic separation, chromatography).
  3. Mixtures are either homogeneous (uniform throughout, e.g. salt water, air, brass) or heterogeneous (non-uniform, parts visible, e.g. muddy water, sand + iron filings, oil + water).

Rounding off Data

Chapter 1 · Lesson 4 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 9–11 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Your calculator might show a mass as 14.36842 g — but your balance can only read to two decimal places. Reporting all those extra digits is fake precision; it pretends you measured more accurately than you really did.

Rounding off trims a number down to the digits you can actually trust, while keeping it as accurate as possible. Every scientist must do it correctly.

📖 Core Concept: Significant Figures

📚 Definition

Rounding off data is a method of reducing the figures from a given numerical value. It means to reduce a number to the desired significant figures and adjust the last reported digit.

First, you need to know which digits are significant (the meaningful, trusted ones). Here are the rules:

RuleExampleSignificant figures
All non-zero digits are significant45824
Zeros between non-zero digits are significant10024
Zeros to the left of the first non-zero digit are not significant0.00252
Final zeros to the right of a decimal point are significant2.5004

The Rules for Rounding Off

Once you know how many figures to keep, look at the first digit you are dropping:

  1. If the digit to be dropped is greater than 5 → add 1 to the digit you keep.
    5.768 → 5.77 (keeping 3 figures; drop 8, so 6 becomes 7)
  2. If the digit to be dropped is less than 5 → keep the previous digit unchanged.
    5.734 → 5.73 (keeping 3 figures; drop 4, leave 3)
  3. If the digit to be dropped is exactly 5 → look at the digit being kept:
    • If it is even, leave it as it is → 7.865 → 7.86 (6 is even)
    • If it is odd, add 1 to it → 23.35 → 23.4 (3 is odd → 4)
🔢
The "exactly 5" rule

When the dropped digit is exactly 5, we round so the kept digit becomes even. This "round-to-even" trick stops errors from piling up in one direction over many calculations.

You can also round to the nearest ten, hundred, thousand, and so on, using the same idea of looking at the next digit.

💡 Quick Examples

NumberRound to 3 sig. figs.Why

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Round each step, then round again at the end."

❌ Rounding too early introduces error. Keep extra digits during the calculation and only round the final answer to the required significant figures.

⚠️ "Leading zeros count as significant."

❌ In 0.0025 the leading zeros only show the position of the decimal point — they are not significant. This number has just 2 significant figures.

⚠️ "When the dropped digit is 5, always round up."

✅ Not in this book's rule. When it is exactly 5, round so the kept digit becomes even (7.865 → 7.86, not 7.87).

Short Questions

Decide first, then reveal.

Q4. Round 0.0231 to two significant figures.

Answer: The significant digits are 2, 3, 1. Keeping two and dropping the 1 (which is < 5) gives 0.023.

Q5. Why must we round off experimental data?

Answer: To remove uncertain (untrustworthy) digits and report a result only as precisely as it was actually measured, ensuring maximum accuracy without false precision.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Rounding off reduces a number to the significant figures you can trust and adjusts the last digit.
  2. Dropped digit > 5 → round up; < 5 → leave; exactly 5 → make the kept digit even.
  3. Leading zeros are not significant; round only the final answer, never mid-calculation.

Limiting Reactant

Chapter 1 · Lesson 6 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 13–15 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Imagine making sandwiches. You have 20 slices of bread and only 8 shami kebabs. Each sandwich needs 2 slices and 1 shami. You can only make 8 sandwiches — then the shamis run out, even though 4 slices of bread are left over.

The shamis limited how many sandwiches you made. Chemical reactions work exactly the same way: one reactant runs out first and decides how much product you get. That one is the limiting reactant.

📖 Core Concept: Limiting vs Excess

📚 Definition

The reactant which is completely consumed while some amount of other reactant remains unreacted is called the limiting reactant (or limiting reagent). It controls the amount of product formed.

✅ Easy Version

The limiting reactant is the one that runs out first. Once it is gone, the reaction stops — no matter how much of the other reactant is still sitting there. That leftover reactant is called the excess reactant.

The 4-Step Method to Find It

  1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
  2. Find the number of moles of each reactant (mass ÷ molar mass).
  3. Find the moles of product each reactant could make, using the mole ratio.
  4. The reactant that makes the least product is the limiting reactant.
🎯
Always use the limiting reactant for the answer

Once you know which reactant is limiting, the amount of product is calculated only from that reactant — never from the excess one.

💡 Worked Examples

Example 1.15 2.8 g of ethene (C2H4) is ignited with 6.4 g of O2. Identify the limiting reactant and find the mass of CO2 formed.
Step 1 — balanced equation
C2H4 + 3O2→2CO2 + 2H2O
Step 2 — moles of each reactant
Moles C2H4 = 2.828 = 0.1   ·   Moles O2 = 6.432 = 0.2
Step 3 — CO2 from each reactant

From C2H4: 1 mole → 2 moles CO2, so 0.1 → 0.2 mol CO2.

From O2: 3 moles → 2 moles CO2, so 0.2 → (2/3) × 0.2 = 0.133 mol CO2.

Step 4 — pick the smaller

O2 produces less CO2 (0.133 < 0.2), so O2 is the limiting reactant.

Mass of CO2 = 0.133 × 44
AnswerLimiting reactant = O2  ·  Mass of CO2 = 5.852 g
Example 1.16 67.5 g of aluminium reacts with 140 g of nitrogen to form aluminium nitride. Find (a) the limiting reactant, (b) the mass of AlN produced, (c) the mass of excess reactant. (Al = 27, N = 14)
Step 1 — balanced equation
2Al + N2→2AlN
Step 2 — moles of each reactant
Moles Al = 67.527 = 2.5   ·   Moles N2 = 14028 = 5
Step 3 — AlN from each reactant

From Al: 2 moles → 2 moles AlN, so 2.5 → 2.5 mol AlN.

From N2: 1 mole → 2 moles AlN, so 5 → 10 mol AlN.

Step 4 — pick the smaller

Al produces less AlN (2.5 < 10), so Al is the limiting reactant and N2 is in excess.

(b) Mass of AlN (molar mass = 41 g/mol)
Mass of AlN = 2.5 × 41 = 102.5 g
(c) Excess N2 left over

2 moles Al need 1 mole N2, so 2.5 moles Al use 1.25 moles N2. Excess N2 = 5 − 1.25 = 3.75 moles → 3.75 × 28 = 105 g.

Answer(a) Al is limiting · (b) 102.5 g AlN · (c) 105 g of N2 left unreacted

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "The reactant with the smaller mass is limiting."

❌ Not necessarily. You must compare moles of product, not masses. A reactant can be smaller in grams yet still produce more product because of its mole ratio.

⚠️ "The reactant with fewer moles is always limiting."

❌ Only true when the mole ratio is 1 : 1. Always run the moles through the balanced equation first — the coefficients decide how much product each reactant can actually make.

⚠️ "Use the excess reactant to find the product."

✅ Never. Product is always calculated from the limiting reactant, because the reaction stops the moment it runs out.

Short Questions

Reason it out first, then reveal.

Q4. Define limiting reactant and excess reactant.

Answer: The limiting reactant is completely used up and controls how much product forms. The excess reactant is the one that is not fully consumed and remains left over after the reaction stops.

Q5. In Example 1.15, why is the CO2 calculated from O2 and not from C2H4?

Answer: Because O2 produces fewer moles of CO2 (0.133 vs 0.2), it runs out first and is the limiting reactant. The reaction stops when O2 is gone, so the product depends on it.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. The limiting reactant runs out first and controls the amount of product; the leftover is the excess reactant.
  2. Find it in 4 steps: balance → moles of each reactant → product from each → smallest wins.
  3. Always calculate the product (and excess) from the limiting reactant — never from the excess one.

Mole Conversions

Chapter 1 · Lesson 2 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 12–15 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

In the lab you can weigh a substance (grams) but you cannot directly count its atoms or measure how many it makes as a gas. The mole is the bridge that connects all three — mass, number of particles, and volume.

Master these three little conversions and you can answer almost any "how much?" question in chemistry. This is the most useful skill in the whole chapter.

📖 Core Concept: The Mole Is the Hub

Everything connects through the mole. To go from one quantity to another, you almost always pass through moles first:

        MASS (g)                NUMBER OF PARTICLES
            \                         /
       ÷ molar mass            ÷ Avogadro's no.
              \                     /
               \                   /
                +----- MOLES -----+
                       |
                  ÷ 22.4 dm³
                       |
                  VOLUME (gas at STP)
Fig 2.1 — The mole sits at the centre. Convert anything ↔ anything by going through moles.

The Three Conversion Formulas

No. of moles = Given mass (g)Molar mass (g/mol)
No. of moles = No. of particlesAvogadro's number (6.02 × 1023)
No. of moles = Volume of gas at STP (dm3)Molar volume (22.4 dm3)
🔄
Reverse them too

Each formula works both ways. Rearranged: mass = moles × molar mass,  particles = moles × 6.02 × 1023,  volume = moles × 22.4.

💡 Worked Examples

A · Mass ↔ Moles

Example 1.1 Calculate the number of moles in 25.5 g of sodium metal.
Given

Mass of sodium = 25.5 g  ·  Molar mass of Na = 23 g/mol

Solution
Moles of Na = Given massMolar mass = 25.5 g23 g/mol
Answer= 1.11 moles of sodium
Example 1.2 Calculate the mass of 3.25 moles of water (H2O).
Given

Moles of water = 3.25  ·  Molar mass of H2O = 18 g/mol

Solution

Rearrange the formula: mass = moles × molar mass

Mass of H2O = 3.25 × 18
Answer= 58.5 g of water

B · Mass ↔ Number of Particles

Example 1.3 Calculate the number of molecules in 610 g of benzoic acid (C7H6O2).
Given

Mass = 610 g  ·  Molar mass of C7H6O2 = (7×12) + (6×1) + (2×16) = 122 g/mol

Step 1 — find moles
Moles = 610122 = 5 moles
Step 2 — moles → molecules
Molecules = 5 × 6.02 × 1023
Answer= 3.01 × 1024 molecules of benzoic acid

C · Number of Particles ↔ Moles

Example 1.5 Calculate the number of moles in 2.35 × 1025 atoms of aluminium (Al).
Solution

Since 6.02 × 1023 atoms = 1 mole:

Moles of Al = 2.35 × 10256.02 × 1023
Answer= 39 moles of aluminium

D · Moles ↔ Volume of a Gas (at STP)

Example 1.6 What volume does 1.5 moles of oxygen gas (O2) occupy at STP?
Solution

At STP, 1 mole of any gas = 22.4 dm3, so:

Volume of O2 = moles × 22.4 = 1.5 × 22.4
Answer= 33.6 dm3 of oxygen at STP

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "I can jump straight from grams to molecules."

❌ Not in one step. Always go mass → moles → particles. The mole is the middle bridge; skipping it is where most mistakes happen.

⚠️ "22.4 dm³ works for any volume question."

❌ Only for gases at STP. If the gas is not at STP, or it is a liquid/solid, this formula does not apply.

⚠️ "Multiply when I should divide."

✅ Quick check: going to moles you divide (mass ÷ molar mass). Coming from moles you multiply (moles × molar mass). If your answer looks far too big or too small, you probably swapped them.

Q4. Calculate the number of moles in 36 g of water (H2O).

Answer: Moles = 36 ÷ 18 = 2 moles of water.

Q5. How many atoms are present in 0.25 mole of sodium?

Answer: Atoms = moles × NA = 0.25 × 6.02 × 1023 = 1.505 × 1023 atoms.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Always convert through moles: mass ↔ moles ↔ particles, and moles ↔ volume.
  2. The three formulas: moles = mass ÷ molar mass = particles ÷ NA = volume ÷ 22.4 (gas at STP).
  3. Divide to reach moles, multiply to leave moles.

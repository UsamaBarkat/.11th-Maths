Physical and Chemical Properties of Matter

Chapter 1 · Lesson 2 of 7 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 12–15 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Think about iron and gold. Both are shiny metals. But leave iron outside for a week — it rusts. Leave gold outside for a year — nothing happens. Why?

The answer is properties of matter. Every substance has two kinds of properties: ones you can observe without changing it, and ones that only show up when it reacts or transforms. Knowing these properties is how chemists identify, use, and separate materials in real life.

📖 Core Concept

📚 Textbook Definition

The properties of matter are divided into two types: Physical Properties (observed without changing the substance's composition) and Chemical Properties (observed only when the substance undergoes a chemical reaction).

✅ Easy Version

Physical property = what you can see, feel, or measure without "breaking" the substance.
Chemical property = how the substance behaves when it reacts and becomes something new.

📚 Definition

A physical property is a characteristic that can be observed or measured without changing the chemical composition of the substance.

2. Chemical Properties

📚 Definition

A chemical property is a characteristic that describes a substance's ability to change into a different substance through a chemical reaction.

Chemical PropertyWhat it meansExample
FlammabilityAbility to burn (react with oxygen)Petrol burns easily; water does not burn
Corrosion / RustingReaction with oxygen or moistureIron rusts; gold does not corrode
Reactivity with AcidHow it reacts with acidsZinc reacts with HCl to produce H₂ gas; gold does not

CombustibilityAbility to undergo combustionCarbon burns in oxygen to form CO₂
ToxicityAbility to poison or harm living thingsCarbon monoxide is highly toxic

3. Physical Changes vs Chemical Changes

📚 Physical Change

A change in form or appearance only — the chemical composition does not change. The substance can often be returned to its original form.

📚 Chemical Change

A change that produces a new substance with different properties. The original substance cannot easily be recovered.

FeaturePhysical ChangeChemical Change
Composition changes?❌ No✅ Yes
New substance formed?❌ No✅ Yes
Reversible?Usually ✅ YesUsually ❌ No
Energy change?SmallLarge (heat/light released or absorbed)
ExampleMelting ice, cutting paper, dissolving saltBurning wood, rusting iron, cooking an egg

Signs That a Chemical Change Has Occurred

  • 🔥 Heat or light produced — burning wood
  • 💨 Gas given off (bubbles) — zinc + acid → hydrogen gas
  • 🟫 Color change — iron turning orange-brown (rust)
  • ⬇️ Precipitate formed — mixing two clear liquids → solid forms
  • 👃 Odor change — food going bad produces new smell

💡 Real-Life Examples

  1. Melting butter 🧈 — Physical change. Butter changes from solid to liquid but is still butter. Cool it down and it becomes solid again.
  2. Burning a matchstick 🔥 — Chemical change. Wood and sulfur react with oxygen to form ash, CO₂, and water vapor. You can never get the matchstick back.
  3. Cutting onions 🧅 — Physical change. You change the shape but onion is still onion chemically.
  4. Milk turning sour 🥛 — Chemical change. Bacteria convert lactose (milk sugar) into lactic acid. A new substance is formed — you cannot un-sour milk.
  5. Dissolving sugar in tea ☕ — Physical change. Sugar molecules spread through water but are still sugar — taste it to confirm!
  6. Rusting of an iron gate 🔩 — Chemical change. Iron reacts with oxygen and water to form iron oxide (rust) — a completely new substance with different properties.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ Confusion #1: "Dissolving is a chemical change"

❌ No! Dissolving salt in water is a physical change. The salt is still salt — evaporate the water and you get the salt crystals back. No new substance was formed.

⚠️ Confusion #2: "If color changes, it must be chemical"

❌ Not always. Mixing blue and yellow paint gives green — this is a physical change (no new substance). But iron turning to rust (orange-brown) IS chemical because a new substance (iron oxide) forms.

⚠️ Confusion #3: "Physical property = something you can only see"

❌ Physical properties include things you measure too — density, melting point, boiling point, electrical conductivity. You don't have to just observe with eyes.

🔑 Quick Memory Trick

Physical = can you get the original substance back? If YES → physical.
Chemical = is a new substance formed? If YES → chemical.

Q5. What is the difference between a physical property and a chemical property? Give one example of each.

Answer:
A physical property can be observed without changing the substance's composition. Example: the melting point of ice (0°C) — ice becomes water but it is still H₂O.

A chemical property describes how a substance reacts to form a new substance. Example: flammability of petrol — when petrol burns it produces CO₂ and water, which are completely different substances.

Q6. Identify whether the following are physical or chemical changes: (a) boiling water, (b) frying an egg, (c) cutting hair.

Answer:
(a) Boiling water — Physical change. Water turns to steam but is still H₂O. Cool it and you get water back.
(b) Frying an egg — Chemical change. Heat causes proteins in the egg to denature permanently — a new substance is formed. You cannot un-fry an egg.
(c) Cutting hair — Physical change. Only the shape/length changes; the chemical composition of hair does not change.

Q7. List any four signs that indicate a chemical change has taken place.

Answer: Signs of a chemical change:
1. Heat or light is produced — e.g. burning paper produces flame and heat.
2. Gas is given off — e.g. zinc reacting with HCl produces hydrogen gas (bubbles).
3. Color change — e.g. iron turning orange-red due to rust formation.
4. Precipitate formed — e.g. mixing two solutions produces a solid that settles at the bottom.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Physical properties (color, density, melting point, boiling point, etc.) are observed without changing the substance's composition. Chemical properties (flammability, reactivity, corrosion) are seen only when the substance undergoes a reaction.
  2. Physical changes change the form but not the composition — reversible (melting ice). Chemical changes produce entirely new substances and are usually irreversible (burning, rusting, cooking).
  3. Signs of a chemical change: heat/light produced, gas given off, color change, precipitate formed, or odor change.

The Story of the Atom

Chapter 2 · Lesson 1 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 8–10 min read

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Take a piece of chalk and break it in half. Break it again. And again. Keep going. Is there a point where you cannot break it any further?

Two Greek thinkers asked exactly this question 2,400 years ago — and their answer was "yes". They called that final, unbreakable piece atomos. They had no microscope, no laboratory, no experiment. Just an argument.

It took humanity until the 1930s to finally look inside that "unbreakable" piece and find that it is not unbreakable at all — it is made of three smaller particles. This lesson is the story of how we got there. Everything else in this chapter builds on it.

📖 Core Concept: From Philosophy to Science

1. The Greeks — an idea with no evidence (440 B.C)

In history, the first time the idea of the atom appeared was with two Greek philosophers, Leucippus and Democritus (440 B.C). They proposed that matter is composed of invisible, indivisible and infinite building blocks.

📚 Where the word comes from

They named these blocks atomos — from Greek a = "non" and tom = "break". So atomos literally means "cannot be broken".

They said that different atoms, and their different combinations, produce the different types of matter we see (solids, liquids and gases).

⚖️
Important: this was NOT science

This ancient theory of the atom was based on philosophical reasoning rather than scientific basis. There was no experiment behind it — only thinking. That is exactly why it was ignored for the next 2,000 years.

2. Aristotle — the idea that won instead

Most philosophers of that time did not give weightage to Leucippus and Democritus' concepts about matter and its composition. Instead they favoured the Aristotelian concept:

📚 Aristotle's view

"Matter is composed of four elements — earth, water, air and fire."

Because Aristotle was so influential, this four-element idea dominated for roughly two thousand years, and the atom was largely forgotten.

3. John Dalton — the atom becomes scientific (1803)

The idea of the atom was revisited and studied upon by many scientists and philosophers. However, it was John Dalton (1803) who is recognised as the introducer of modern atomic theory. With Dalton, the atom stopped being a philosophical guess and became a scientific theory.

The road from a Greek idea to the modern atom

🔬 The Three Discoveries That Built the Modern Atom

Millikan's Oil Drop Experiment (1909)

After 12 years of the discovery of the electron as a fundamental entity that dwells in an atom, Robert Andrews Millikan, in 1909, performed an ingenious oil drop experiment. He suspended a charged oil drop in an electric field so that its weight was balanced by the electrostatic force.

He found that the charge on every oil drop was an integral multiple of 1.6 × 10−19 coulomb — which encouraged him to deduce that this must be the charge of Thomson's electrons.

💡
Why "integral multiple" matters

Every drop carried 1×, 2×, 3× … that charge — never 1.5× or 2.3×. Charge comes in fixed packets. That packet is the electron's charge. This is the same "quantised" thinking you will meet again in Bohr's model.

Rutherford and the Nucleus (1911)

In 1911, Ernest Rutherford laid the foundation that the atom consists of an extremely charged region which he called the nucleus, wherein most of the mass of an atom is concentrated, while electrons revolve in the extra-nuclear part.

Chadwick and the Neutron (1932)

By 1920, scientists knew and believed that most of the mass of the atom was located in the central nuclear core. Then in 1932, the English chemist James Chadwick informed the world about the third sub-atomic particle — one that was neutral and a part of the nucleus — which he named the neutron.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Breaking chalk 🖍️ — Democritus' whole argument was "you must eventually hit a piece you cannot break". That final piece is the atom.
  2. An idea vs a proof 🧾 — The Greeks had the right answer for the wrong reason (no evidence). Science needs both. That is why we credit Dalton, not Democritus, with the modern theory.
  3. Coins in a wallet 🪙 — Millikan found charge always comes in whole packets of 1.6 × 10−19 C, just like money in a shop always comes in whole rupees — never half a paisa.
  4. Finding the seed in a fruit 🥭 — Rutherford showed the atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, heavy centre — like a mango that is mostly flesh with one dense stone in the middle.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Democritus discovered the atom."

❌ He proposed the idea of the atom — he did not discover it. His work was philosophical reasoning with no experiment. Dalton (1803) is credited as the introducer of the modern (scientific) atomic theory.

⚠️ "The proton was discovered after the electron."

❌ Careful — this trips up nearly everyone. The proton came first (1886, Goldstein) and the electron second (1897, Thomson). The neutron was last (1932, Chadwick). Remember the order: P → E → N.

⚠️ "Millikan discovered the electron."

❌ Thomson discovered the electron (1897). Millikan measured its charge (1909) using the oil drop experiment. Two different scientists, two different achievements.

⚠️ "Atoms really are indivisible, like the Greeks said."

❌ The name atomos ("cannot be broken") turned out to be wrong. Atoms can be broken into electrons, protons and neutrons — and the nucleus itself can split in a fission reaction. We simply kept the old name.

Q5. Why was the Greek atomic theory not accepted as science?

Answer: Because it was based on philosophical reasoning rather than a scientific basis. Leucippus and Democritus had no experiment or evidence to support the idea, so most philosophers of that time ignored it and favoured Aristotle's four-element concept instead.

Q6. What did Rutherford conclude in 1911?

Answer: That the atom consists of an extremely charged central region called the nucleus, where most of the mass of the atom is concentrated, while the electrons revolve in the extra-nuclear part.

Q7. Arrange the three sub-atomic particles in the order they were discovered, with dates.

Answer: Proton — Goldstein, 1886 A.D → Electron — J.J. Thomson, 1897 A.D → Neutron — James Chadwick, 1932 A.D.

📝 Summary

4 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Leucippus and Democritus (440 B.C) proposed that matter is made of invisible, indivisible blocks called atomos — but only by philosophical reasoning, not experiment. Aristotle's rival "four elements" idea won instead.
  2. John Dalton (1803) is recognised as the introducer of modern atomic theory.
  3. Order of discovery: proton (1886, Goldstein) → electron (1897, Thomson) → neutron (1932, Chadwick). In between, Millikan (1909) found the electron's charge = 1.6 × 10−19 C and Rutherford (1911) found the nucleus.
  4. Bohr (1913) proposed the idea of energy levels for electrons — which is where the next few lessons are heading.

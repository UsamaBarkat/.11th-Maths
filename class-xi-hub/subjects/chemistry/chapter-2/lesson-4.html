Radius & Energy of an Electron

Chapter 2 · Lesson 4 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 15–18 min read Topic 2.2.1

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Bohr did not just say "electrons live in fixed orbits". He did something far bolder — he calculated exactly how big those orbits are and exactly how much energy each one holds, using nothing but Coulomb's law and his own quantization rule.

And the numbers came out right. That is why the theory was believed.

This lesson has two derivations. They look frightening, but each one is just a chain of substitutions. Follow the chain once and you will be able to reproduce it — and the two final formulas are short enough to memorise in a minute.

📖 The Setup: Two Forces in Balance

The truth of the basic assumption of Bohr's theory was established when it was applied to single electron systems like hydrogen and ions such as He+1, Li+2 etc. A hydrogen atom consists of a single electron revolving around the nucleus.

              ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─
           ─                   ─          v (velocity)
         ─                       ─        ↑
        │          r             │        │
        │   (Ze) ─────────── (e⁻, mass m)
        │                        │
         ─                     ─
           ─                 ─
              ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─ ─

   Ze = charge on nucleus      e = charge on electron
   m  = mass of electron       r = radius of the orbit
Fig 2.2 — the hydrogen atom

Let e and m be the charge and mass of the electron, Ze the charge on the nucleus, and r the radius of the orbit in which the electron moves with velocity v.

Force 1 — the electrostatic pull (Coulomb's law)

F = K Ze+ e−r2

Where K is the proportionality constant. It is equal to 1 / 4πεo. Hence the attractive force between nucleus and electron is written as:

F = Ze24πεor2

Force 2 — the centrifugal force

At the same time, the centrifugal force on the electron will be:

mv2r
⚖️
The key idea of the whole derivation

As far as the electron remains in the same orbit, the two opposite forces are equal. The inward pull exactly balances the outward push — that is what keeps the orbit stable. Everything else follows from setting them equal.

🧮 Derivation 1: Radius of the nth Orbit

Derivation Show that r = aon2 / Z
Step 1 — equate the two forces
mv2r = Ze24πεor2
Step 2 — cancel one r from each side
mv2 = Ze24πεor   — — — — (i)
Step 3 — make r the subject
r = Ze24πεomv2   — — — — (ii)
Step 4 — bring in Bohr's postulate (iv)
mvr = nh2π   ⟹   v = nh2πmr
Step 5 — take the square on both sides
v2 = n2h24π2m2r2
Step 6 — put v2 into equation (ii)
r = Ze2 · 4π2m2r24πεom n2h2
Step 7 — rearrange
r = εon2h2Zπme2   — — — — (iii)
Step 8 — bundle the constants

Everything except n and Z is a constant. Assembling all constants in equation (iii):

r = aon2Z   — — — — (iv)   where   ao = εoh2πme2

The constants you are given

SymbolNameValue
εoVacuum permittivity constant8.84 × 10−12 C2/J·m
hPlanck's constant6.625 × 10−34 J·s
mMass of electron9.11 × 10−31 kg
eCharge of electron1.602 × 10−19 C
aoBohr's constant0.529 × 10−8 cm = 0.529 Å
💡
What equation (iv) is for

Equation (iv) is used for the determination of the radius of the nth orbit of the hydrogen atom and hydrogen-like ions viz. He+, Li+2 etc. Notice the shape of it: radius grows as n2 and shrinks as Z grows. A bigger nuclear charge pulls the electron in tighter.

📌
A note on ao

ao (Bohr's constant) is a pure constant — its value is always 0.529 Å, whatever the element. The Z appears in equation (iv), not inside ao. If you see a Z written into the ao expression in some printings, treat it as a misprint; using 0.529 Å with r = aon2/Z always gives the right answer.

Example 2.1 Find the radius of the 4th orbit of an electron in a hydrogen atom.
Step 1 — write the formula

The radius of n = 4 for the hydrogen atom can be determined by using Bohr's equation.

r = aon2Z
Step 2 — substitute (for hydrogen, Z = 1)
r = 0.529 × (4)21
Step 3 — solve
r = 0.529 × 16 = 8.46 Å

⚡ Derivation 2: Energy of an Electron in the nth Orbit

In any orbit of an atom, the total energy of an electron is the sum of potential energy (P.E) and kinetic energy (K.E).

Etotal = K.E + P.E
Derivation Show that E = −K(1/n2)
Step 1 — find the potential energy

P.E is the work done in bringing the electron from infinity to a point at a distance r from the nucleus:

P·E = work done = − force × displacement
P·E = −Ze24πεor2 × r   ⟹   P·E = −Ze24πεor

Here the negative sign indicates that P.E decreases when the electron is brought from infinity to a point at distance r.

Step 2 — write the total energy
Etotal = ( ½mv2 ) + ( −Ze24πεor )
E = [ ½mv2 ] − [ Ze24πεor ]   — — — — (v)
Step 3 — replace mv2 using equation (i)

From the force balance, mv2 = Ze2 / 4πεor. Insert it into (v):

E = ½ [ Ze24πεor ] − [ Ze24πεor ]
Step 4 — simplify (½ of a thing minus the whole thing = −½ of it)
E = −Ze28πεor   — — — — (vi)
Step 5 — substitute r from equation (iii)
E = −Ze28πεo × πmZe2εon2h2
E = −mZ2e48εo2n2h2   — — — — (vii)
Step 6 — for hydrogen, Z = 1
E = −me48εo2h2 ( 1n2 )   — — — — (viii)
Step 7 — bundle the constants into K
E = −K ( 1n2 )   — — — — (ix)

Here K is a factor assembled by the various constants present in equation (viii). Its value is:

K = 2.18 × 10−18 J/atom   or   1312.8 kJ/mol
🔑
Why the energy is negative

The minus sign is not a mistake — it means the electron is bound to the nucleus. Zero energy is defined as the electron sitting at infinity, completely free. Any electron inside the atom has less energy than that, so its value is negative. And since E ∝ −1/n2, the electron is most tightly bound at n = 1 (most negative) and gets closer to zero as n rises.

Example 2.2 Calculate the energy of an electron in the L-shell of a hydrogen atom (the value of K is 2.178 × 10−18 J/atom).
Step 1 — identify n

The L-shell corresponds to the second energy level (n = 2).

Step 2 — Bohr's formula for energy
E = −Kn2
Step 3 — substitute
E = −2.18 × 10−18(2)2
Step 4 — solve
E = −5.45 × 10−19 joule
Note on the printed answer

The textbook prints this answer as −5.43 × 10−19 J. Dividing 2.18 × 10−18 by 4 actually gives −5.45 × 10−19 J (and using 2.178 gives −5.445 × 10−19 J). The difference is only rounding — write the full working and either value will be accepted.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Swinging a stone on a string 🪨 — The string pulls inward, the stone pushes outward, and it circles steadily. Cut the string and it flies off. The electrostatic force is the electron's string.
  2. Orbits spread out fast 📏 — Because r ∝ n2, the 4th orbit is 16 times bigger than the first, not 4 times. The outer shells are far more spread out than students expect.
  3. A well, not a hill 🕳️ — Negative energy means the electron sits in a "well". Getting it out takes work. Deeper (n = 1) means harder to remove — that is why the first ionisation energy is the biggest.
  4. Bigger Z pulls harder 🧲 — In He+ (Z = 2) the same orbit is half the radius of hydrogen's, because the nucleus pulls twice as hard.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "The radius doubles when n doubles."

❌ Radius depends on n2, not n. Going from n = 2 to n = 4 multiplies the radius by 4, not 2.

⚠️ "Energy increases as n increases, so it becomes more positive and larger."

⚠️ Energy does increase with n — but it stays negative, moving towards zero. −2.18 × 10−18 J (n = 1) is lower than −5.45 × 10−19 J (n = 2), even though the second number looks smaller in magnitude.

⚠️ "K in E = −K/n² is the same K as in Coulomb's law."

❌ Two completely different constants that unfortunately share a letter. In Coulomb's law K = 1/4πεo. In the energy equation K = 2.18 × 10−18 J/atom, a bundle of constants from equation (viii). Read from context.

⚠️ "0.529 works in every formula as it is."

⚠️ Watch your units. ao = 0.529 Å = 0.529 × 10−8 cm = 0.529 × 10−10 m. If the question asks for metres, convert — otherwise your answer is out by a factor of 1010.

⚠️ "These formulas work for any atom."

❌ Only for single-electron systems: H, He+, Li+2, Be+3. For neutral helium or anything bigger, Bohr's equations fail — which is Lesson 6's topic.

Numerical & Short Questions

Q5. Find the radius of the 3rd orbit of a hydrogen atom.

Answer: r = aon2/Z = (0.529 × 32)/1 = 0.529 × 9 = 4.761 Å.

Q6. Find the radius of the 2nd orbit of He+ (Z = 2).

Answer: r = aon2/Z = (0.529 × 22)/2 = (0.529 × 4)/2 = 1.058 Å. Note it is half the hydrogen value for the same n, because Z is twice as large.

Q7. Calculate the energy of an electron in the K-shell of a hydrogen atom.

Answer: The K-shell is n = 1. E = −K/n2 = −2.18 × 10−18/(1)2 = −2.18 × 10−18 joule. This is the lowest (most negative) energy an electron in hydrogen can have — the ground state.

Q8. Why are the two forces on the electron set equal in Bohr's derivation?

Answer: Because as long as the electron remains in the same orbit, the orbit is stable — so the inward electrostatic force of attraction (Ze2/4πεor2) must be exactly balanced by the outward centrifugal force (mv2/r). Equating them is what makes the radius calculable.

📝 Summary

5 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. The orbit is stable because the electrostatic force = centrifugal force: Ze2/4πεor2 = mv2/r.
  2. Combining that with mvr = nh/2π gives r = εon2h2 / Zπme2, which bundles into r = aon2/Z with ao = 0.529 Å.
  3. Total energy = K.E + P.E leads to E = −Ze2/8πεor, and substituting r gives E = −mZ2e4 / 8εo2n2h2.
  4. For hydrogen this simplifies to E = −K/n2 with K = 2.18 × 10−18 J/atom = 1312.8 kJ/mol.
  5. Worked results to remember: r4(H) = 8.46 Å and EL-shell(H) ≈ −5.45 × 10−19 J.

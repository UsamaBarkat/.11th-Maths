Bohr's Atomic Theory

Chapter 2 · Lesson 3 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 10–12 min read Topic 2.2

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Rutherford gave us a beautiful picture: a tiny positive nucleus with electrons circling it, just like planets around the sun. It is the picture most people still have in their heads today.

There is only one problem. According to the physics of the time, that atom should collapse in a fraction of a second. Every atom in your body should have destroyed itself long ago.

In 1913 a Danish physicist named Niels Bohr fixed it — not by changing the picture, but by adding one radical rule: electrons are only allowed in certain orbits, and nowhere in between. This lesson is that fix.

📖 Core Concept: Why Rutherford's Model Failed

Rutherford became successful in presenting that atoms have a tiny positive nucleus and negatively charged electrons revolve around it. He compared the movement of electrons around the nucleus to planets around the sun.

⚠️
The flaw in the solar-system comparison

His comparison of the atomic model with the solar system was defective, because electrons are charged bodies while planets are charge-less. A moving charge behaves very differently from a moving rock.

The two problems this created

❌ Problem 1 — the atom should collapse

If electrons revolve like planets, they should lose energy continuously. As a result, electrons would become closer and closer to the nucleus and finally fall into it.

❌ Problem 2 — the wrong kind of spectrum

Further, if an electron continuously emits energy, it should form a continuous spectrum.

But actually, both phenomena did not happen. Neither do electrons fall into the nucleus, nor is a continuous spectrum formed. On the contrary, a line spectrum is formed.

Rutherford's model contradicted two observed facts

These complications were explained by the Danish physicist Niels Bohr (1913 A.D). His atomic model was based on Planck's quantum theory.

📜 The Four Postulates of Bohr's Theory

These four postulates are a guaranteed exam question. Learn them in order.

📚 Postulate (i) — fixed orbits

Electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits situated at a fixed distance from the nucleus, with definite energy.

📚 Postulate (ii) — no energy change inside an orbit

As long as an electron remains in an appropriate orbit, it neither loses nor gains energy. Hence each orbit has a fixed energy level; however, the energy of orbits increases with increased distance from the nucleus.

📚 Postulate (iii) — jumping between orbits

During excitation, an electron absorbs some quantized energy and jumps from a lower energy orbit to an appropriate higher energy orbit. But when it returns back to the lower energy orbit, it emits quantized energy.

ΔE = E2 − E1 = hυ

Here υ is the frequency of radiation and h is Planck's constant.

📚 Postulate (iv) — quantized angular momentum

An electron can move only in those orbits in which the angular momentum of the electron (mvr) is an integral multiple of nh/2π.

mvr = nh2π

Here m and v are the mass and velocity of the electron, and r is the radius of the orbit.

🔑
What "quantized" actually means

Quantized = only certain fixed values are allowed, nothing in between. An electron can have the energy of orbit 1 or orbit 2 — but never anything halfway. This single idea, borrowed from Planck, is what saves the atom from collapsing, and it is the reason postulate (iv) only permits whole-number values of n.

🪜 How the Four Postulates Fit Together

   ENERGY
     ↑
     |  ──────────────  n = 4        ← higher orbit = higher energy
     |                                  (postulate ii)
     |  ──────────────  n = 3
     |         ↑   ↓
     |  ──────────────  n = 2       absorb energy → jump UP
     |         ↑   ↓                emit energy   → fall DOWN
     |  ──────────────  n = 1          (postulate iii)
     |
   (nucleus)

   Allowed orbits only: n = 1, 2, 3, 4 …   (postulate i & iv)
   NOTHING is allowed between the lines.
Energy levels are like steps on a ladder — never a ramp
  • Postulate (i) says where the electron may be — fixed circular orbits.
  • Postulate (ii) answers Rutherford's collapse problem: while it stays in its orbit, the electron does not radiate at all. So it never spirals in.
  • Postulate (iii) answers the spectrum problem: energy is only emitted in discrete jumps, so we see separate lines, not a continuous band.
  • Postulate (iv) is the mathematical rule that decides which orbits are allowed — and it is the equation you will actually use to derive the radius in the next lesson.
💡
Where Bohr's theory works

The truth of the basic assumption of Bohr's theory was established when it was applied to single electron systems — like the hydrogen atom and ions such as He+1 and Li+2. That restriction becomes important in Lesson 6, where we look at the model's defects.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Stairs, not a ramp 🪜 — You can stand on step 1 or step 2, never at step 1.5. Electron energies work exactly the same way. That is quantization.
  2. Rent-free floors 🏢 — While an electron "lives" on a floor (orbit), it pays nothing — no energy lost. It only pays (emits) or is paid (absorbs) when it moves between floors.
  3. A satellite that never falls 🛰️ — Rutherford's electron was like a satellite losing altitude every second until it crashes. Bohr's rule is: certain orbits are "safe" and a satellite parked there simply never loses height.
  4. Barcode vs rainbow 🌈 — A continuous spectrum is a smooth rainbow. A line spectrum is a barcode — a few sharp lines with dark gaps. Nature gives us the barcode, and only Bohr's jumping-electron idea explains why.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Rutherford's model was completely wrong."

❌ No — Rutherford was right about the nucleus and right that electrons revolve around it. What was defective was the solar-system comparison, because electrons are charged and planets are not. Bohr kept Rutherford's picture and added the quantization rule on top.

⚠️ "The electron loses energy while revolving in its orbit."

❌ That is exactly what Bohr's postulate (ii) forbids. As long as the electron stays in an allowed orbit, it neither loses nor gains energy. Energy only changes when it jumps between orbits.

⚠️ "Energy is absorbed when the electron falls to a lower orbit."

❌ Reversed. Absorbs energy → jumps up (excitation). Emits energy → returns down. And ΔE = E2 − E1 = hυ in both directions.

⚠️ "n can be any number."

❌ In mvr = nh/2π, the angular momentum must be an integral multiple of h/2π. So n = 1, 2, 3, 4 … — whole numbers only. n = 1.5 is not an allowed orbit.

⚠️ "Bohr's model was based on Rutherford's theory."

⚠️ It corrected Rutherford's model, but the theory it was based on is Planck's quantum theory. Examiners ask this specifically.

Short Questions

Q5. State the four postulates of Bohr's atomic theory.

Answer:
  1. Electrons revolve around the nucleus in circular orbits at a fixed distance with definite energy.
  2. As long as an electron remains in an appropriate orbit it neither loses nor gains energy; each orbit has a fixed energy level, and energy increases with distance from the nucleus.
  3. During excitation the electron absorbs quantized energy and jumps to a higher orbit; on returning it emits quantized energy, where ΔE = E2 − E1 = hυ.
  4. An electron moves only in those orbits where its angular momentum mvr = nh/2π — an integral multiple of h/2π.

Q6. What two things did Rutherford's model wrongly predict?

Answer: (1) That the electron would lose energy continuously, come closer and closer to the nucleus and finally fall into it; and (2) that continuous emission of energy would produce a continuous spectrum. Neither happens — electrons do not fall into the nucleus, and a line spectrum is observed instead.

Q7. What is meant by "quantized energy"?

Answer: Energy that can only take certain fixed, definite values — never values in between. An electron absorbs or emits energy only in whole packets equal to the difference between two orbits, ΔE = hυ. This is why only certain orbits (n = 1, 2, 3 …) are allowed.

📝 Summary

4 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Rutherford's model was defective because it compared charged electrons to charge-less planets — predicting that atoms collapse and give a continuous spectrum. Neither is observed.
  2. Niels Bohr (1913 A.D) resolved this using Planck's quantum theory.
  3. The four postulates: (i) fixed circular orbits with definite energy · (ii) no energy gained or lost within an orbit, energy rises with distance · (iii) ΔE = E2 − E1 = hυ on jumping · (iv) mvr = nh/2π.
  4. The theory holds for single-electron systems: H, He+1, Li+2.

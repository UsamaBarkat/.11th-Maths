Sub-Atomic Particles and Their Characteristics

Chapter 2 · Lesson 2 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 12–14 min read Topic 2.1

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Everything you will ever learn in chemistry — why sodium explodes in water, why gold never rusts, why water boils at 100 °C — comes down to three particles and how they are arranged.

More than a hundred sub-atomic particles have been discovered inside the atom: electron, proton, neutron, positron, mesons, hyperons, neutrino, antineutrino and many more. But you only need three. Only the electron, proton and neutron are considered the fundamental particles of an atom, because only these three determine the physical and chemical properties of elements.

Learn these three properly and the rest of the chapter falls into place.

📖 Core Concept: The Layout of an Atom

An atom has two regions: a tiny central nucleus and a huge extra-nuclear part where the electrons live.

💡
Two facts worth memorising

Almost all of the mass of an atom exists in the nucleus — about 99.94%. And the nucleus was discovered by Rutherford in 1911 A.D.

🧪
The one exception

Except protium (an isotope of hydrogen), the nuclei of all other atoms contain neutrons. Protium's nucleus is a single proton — nothing else.

⚡ Electrons

📚 Discovery

The sub-atomic particle electron was discovered by J.J. Thomson in 1897 A.D, while studying the cathode rays experiment. He called the cathode ray beam "electrons". Thereafter, Millikan with his "Oil Drop Experiment" calculated the charge on an electron.

Properties of the electron

  • Electrons revolve in orbits and occupy about 100,000 times greater volume than the nucleus — but form less than one percent of the total mass of an atom.
  • They carry a negative charge, whose magnitude is equal to the positive charge of protons.
  • Electrons are attracted by protons. This attractive force (the electrostatic force) keeps electrons constantly moving around the nucleus.
  • The mass of an electron is nearly 1836 times less than a proton and 1839 times less than a neutron.
🔑
Why electrons matter more than the nucleus in chemistry

Electronic configuration refers to the presence of electrons in various shells and sub-shells. This arrangement affects the atom's stability, melting point, boiling point and density. Electrons are also the particles involved in chemical bond formation. The nucleus decides which element it is; the electrons decide how it behaves.

➕ Protons

📚 Discovery

Protons are positively charged sub-atomic particles located in the nuclei of all elements. They were discovered by Goldstein (1886 A.D), a German physicist, by using a perforated cathode in Crook's discharge tube.

Goldstein observed that positive ions were formed when cathode rays hit the gaseous atoms in the discharge tube. Rutherford later called these positively charged particles "PROTONS".

Properties of the proton

  • With respect to mass and charge, the protons of all elements are identical to each other.
  • Atomic number of an element is related only to protons.
  • Mass number is the sum of protons and neutrons.
  • Protons and electrons have the same magnitude of charge but different masses.
  • All elements have a different number of protons — this is what makes them different elements.
Atomic number (Z) = number of protons  ·  Mass number (A) = protons + neutrons
🪪
The proton is the atom's identity card

Change the number of neutrons and you get an isotope of the same element. Change the number of electrons and you get an ion of the same element. But change the number of protons and it becomes a completely different element.

⚪ Neutrons

📚 Discovery

The discovery of neutrons was made by James Chadwick (1932 A.D) in an artificial radioactivity experiment. He bombarded alpha particles from Polonium on Beryllium, a stable element, and noted that some highly penetrating neutral particles were produced. These particles were named neutrons.

4Be9 + 2He4 → 6C12 + 0n1  [neutron]

Properties of the neutron

  • Like protons, neutrons are also part of the atomic mass, but the neutron has no charge.
  • Neutrons of all elements have the same nature and mass.
  • They are bound with each other and with protons by the nuclear forces.
  • Neutrons are slightly heavier than protons.
  • They are not deflected by electric or magnetic fields (because they are neutral).
  • Isotopes of the same element always have the same number of protons but a different number of neutrons.
  • The stability of the nucleus depends upon the neutrons.
Number of neutrons = Mass number − Number of protons
💥
When the nucleus breaks

Under certain circumstances, when the electrostatic force (repulsion between protons) overcomes the nuclear force, the nucleus becomes unstable and a fission reaction occurs. Neutrons act as the "glue spacers" that keep protons from pushing each other apart — which is exactly why nuclear stability depends on them.

Electron (e) 0.00055 9.11 × 10−28 −1 −1.602 × 10−19
Proton (p) 1.0073 1.673 × 10−24 +1 +1.602 × 10−19
Neutron (n) 1.0086 1.674 × 10−24 0 0
🧠
How to remember the table

Proton and neutron both sit at about 1 a.m.u — the neutron just a hair heavier (1.0086 > 1.0073). The electron is nowhere near them: about 0.00055 a.m.u, which is roughly 1/1836 of a proton. Charge: −1, +1, 0 — and both charged particles carry exactly 1.602 × 10−19 C, only the sign differs.

🧮 Solved Self-Assessment (from the book)

Self Assessment (i) How many electrons, protons and neutrons are present in 6530Zn?
Step 1 — read the symbol

The lower number is the atomic number Z = 30. The upper number is the mass number A = 65.

Step 2 — protons

Atomic number is related only to protons, so protons = 30.

Step 3 — electrons

The atom is neutral, so electrons must balance the protons: electrons = 30.

Step 4 — neutrons
Neutrons = Mass number − Number of protons = 65 − 30 = 35
Answer

30 electrons, 30 protons and 35 neutrons.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. A stadium and a marble 🏟️ — If the atom were a cricket stadium, the nucleus would be a marble at the centre. Nearly all the mass is in that marble; the electrons are specks flying around the stands.
  2. Weightless but in charge ⚡ — Electrons are under 1% of the mass, yet they do all the chemistry. Like a light switch: tiny compared to the building, but it controls everything.
  3. The ID number 🪪 — Protons are the atom's ID number. 6 protons = carbon, always. 79 protons = gold, always. No exceptions.
  4. Cement between bricks 🧱 — Protons repel each other fiercely. Neutrons sit between them like cement, holding the nucleus together. Too little cement and the wall (nucleus) collapses — that is fission.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Only three sub-atomic particles exist."

❌ More than a hundred sub-atomic particles have been discovered — positron, mesons, hyperons, neutrino, antineutrino and others. But only the electron, proton and neutron are considered the fundamental particles, because only these determine the physical and chemical properties of elements.

⚠️ "Thomson discovered the proton because he did the discharge tube work."

❌ Goldstein (1886) discovered the proton using a perforated cathode in Crook's discharge tube. Thomson (1897) discovered the electron from cathode rays. And it was Rutherford who gave the proton its name.

⚠️ "The proton is heavier than the neutron."

❌ The other way round. Neutrons are slightly heavier than protons — 1.0086 a.m.u vs 1.0073 a.m.u. The difference is small but it is the answer examiners want.

⚠️ "Every atom's nucleus contains neutrons."

❌ Protium (the ordinary isotope of hydrogen) has no neutrons — just one proton. Every other atom's nucleus does contain neutrons.

⚠️ "Mass number and atomic mass are the same thing."

⚠️ Be careful. Mass number is a whole-number count: protons + neutrons. Atomic mass is a measured, usually decimal, value in a.m.u. In this chapter, when you are asked for neutrons, always use mass number − protons.

Short Questions

Q6. Why is the electron considered more important than the nucleus in chemical reactions?

Answer: Because electrons are the particles involved in chemical bond formation. Their arrangement in shells and sub-shells (the electronic configuration) affects atomic stability, melting point, boiling point and density. The nucleus decides which element it is, but the electrons decide how it reacts.

Q7. How does the stability of a nucleus depend on neutrons?

Answer: Neutrons are bound with protons and with each other by nuclear forces, which hold the nucleus together against the repulsion between protons. Under certain circumstances, when the electrostatic force between protons overcomes the nuclear force, the nucleus becomes unstable and a fission reaction occurs.

Q8. Find the number of electrons, protons and neutrons in 2311Na.

Answer: Z = 11, A = 23. Protons = 11; electrons = 11 (neutral atom); neutrons = A − Z = 23 − 11 = 12.

Q9. Write the nuclear reaction Chadwick used to discover the neutron.

Answer: 4Be9 + 2He4 → 6C12 + 0n1 — alpha particles from polonium were bombarded on beryllium, producing highly penetrating neutral particles called neutrons.

📝 Summary

5 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Over a hundred sub-atomic particles exist, but only the electron, proton and neutron are the fundamental particles that decide an element's properties.
  2. Electron — Thomson, 1897 (cathode rays); charge measured by Millikan. Occupies ~100,000× the nucleus's volume but under 1% of the mass; ~1836× lighter than a proton.
  3. Proton — Goldstein, 1886 (perforated cathode, Crook's tube); named by Rutherford. Z = protons, A = protons + neutrons, and ~99.94% of the atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  4. Neutron — Chadwick, 1932 (α from Po on Be). No charge, slightly heavier than a proton, undeflected by fields, and responsible for nuclear stability. Neutrons = A − Z.
  5. Table 2.1: e = 0.00055 a.m.u / −1 / −1.602 × 10−19 C · p = 1.0073 a.m.u / +1 / +1.602 × 10−19 C · n = 1.0086 a.m.u / 0 / 0.

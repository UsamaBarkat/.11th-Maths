Defects of Bohr's Model & Types of Spectra

Chapter 2 · Lesson 6 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 13–15 min read Topics 2.2.2 & 2.2.3

🎯 Why Should You Care?

Bohr's model is beautiful, it predicts hydrogen perfectly, and it won a Nobel Prize. It is also wrong.

Try it on a helium atom — just one extra electron — and it fails completely. Understanding why it fails is what pushes chemistry towards quantum numbers and orbitals, which is where the rest of this chapter goes.

Then we look at the evidence that started the whole argument: spectra. Every element leaves a pattern of coloured lines that is as unique as a fingerprint — and that fingerprint is how we know energy levels are real.

❌ The Defects of Bohr's Theory

Confusions that arose in the Rutherford atomic model were explained in Bohr's atomic theory comprehensively, but there were also some deficiencies in his proposed model.

❌ Defect (i) — only works for one electron

Bohr's model is only applicable to hydrogen and those species which have a single revolving electron around the nucleus (He+, Li2+, Be3+). It could not explain the spectra of multi-electron systems like He, Li, Be, B etc.

❌ Defect (ii) — orbits are not flat circles

According to Bohr's concept, the electron revolves around the nucleus in circular orbits. Later on, it was proved that the electron did not move in a single plane but in three dimensional space.

❌ Defect (iii) — it breaks the uncertainty principle

According to Bohr's concept, the electron in an atom is located at a definite distance from the nucleus and revolves with a definite velocity — which is against the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

❌ Defect (iv) — it ignores the wave nature of the electron

Bohr's theory explains only the particle nature of the electron and did not explain the wave nature of the electron (de Broglie's hypothesis).

🧠
A memory hook for the four defects

Think "One · Flat · Certain · Particle":
One electron only → Flat circular orbits → too Certain about position and velocity → treats the electron only as a Particle.

📌
Two names you must attach correctly

Defect (iii) is linked to Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. Defect (iv) is linked to de Broglie's hypothesis. Examiners frequently swap these two in MCQs.

🌈 Spectrum — the Basics

📚 Monochromatic light

A light which is composed of one kind of rays or wavelengths is known as monochromatic light.

📚 Polychromatic light

Lights like sunlight, bulb light etc. are composed of more than one type of rays. These are known as polychromatic lights.

📚 Spectrum

When a beam of any polychromatic light is passed through a glass prism (placed in a spectrometer), it splits (disperses) into several colours in order of increasing or decreasing wavelengths. The band of colours is called a spectrum.

💡
Do You Know?

The word spectroscopy is derived from the Latin word spectrum, which means image, and the Greek word skopia, which means observation. It is the study of the absorption and emission of light by matter.

🌅 Continuous Spectrum

📚 Definition

The spectrum which contains a continuous band of different colours is known as a continuous spectrum. In this spectrum all colours are diffused into each other and the boundary line between the colours is not marked.

When sunlight passes through a prism, it deviates and splits into a continuous band of seven colours — VIBGYOR.

📊 Line Spectrum

📚 Definition

When light emitted from electrically or thermally excited atoms is passed through a prism, certain distinct lines separated by dark space are obtained. This is known as a line spectrum.

A gas is excited by strongly heating it, or by passing it through an electric discharge tube at low pressure. Each element emits light of a specific wavelength. Therefore the number of lines and the distance between them depends upon the nature of the element.

🔍
The "Finger Print" of an element

Because every element gives its own unique pattern of lines, line spectra are used as a 'Finger Print' for the identification of elements. For example, the line spectrum of sodium contains two yellow coloured lines separated by a definite distance.

What line spectra prove

Line spectra of the elements give the information that electrons around the nucleus have a definite amount of energy and are arranged in definite energy levels. After absorbing energy, an electron in level E1 jumps to an appropriate energy level E2 and then returns back. The difference in emitted energy, E2 − E1, is equal to the energy absorbed.

🔗
This is the evidence for Lessons 3–5

The line spectrum is the experimental proof of Bohr's postulate (iii). If energy levels were not quantized, we would see a smooth rainbow. We see separate lines instead — so the levels must be discrete. The formulas you learned last lesson predict exactly where those lines fall.

A line spectrum can be seen in the following two ways:

  1. Absorption line spectrum
  2. Emission line spectrum

⚫⚪ Absorption vs Emission Line Spectrum

📚 Absorption Line Spectrum

When a beam of light is passed through an absorbing material like a gaseous sample of an element, the element absorbs certain wavelengths while the rest of the wavelengths pass through it. The spectrum obtained consists of a series of dark lines with a bright background.

📚 Emission Line Spectrum

Sodium vapour lamps (street lights), mercury vapour lamps, electrical discharge tubes, hot solids or elements emit radiations of certain wavelengths. The spectrum which is formed from such radiations is called an emission line spectrum.

🪞
They are mirror images

The same element absorbs exactly the wavelengths it would emit. So its absorption spectrum is the photographic negative of its emission spectrum — dark lines sitting precisely where the bright ones would be.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. A rainbow vs a barcode 🌈 — Continuous spectrum = smooth rainbow, colours blending with no boundary. Line spectrum = barcode, sharp lines with dark gaps.
  2. Fingerprints at a crime scene 🔍 — Astronomers identify which elements are in a distant star without ever going there: they read the star's line spectrum like a fingerprint.
  3. Street lights 🟠 — That distinctive orange of an old sodium street lamp is literally sodium's two yellow lines, glowing at you from a metre away.
  4. Bohr's model as a bicycle 🚲 — Perfect for one rider (one electron). Put a second person on it and it falls over. That is the model's first and biggest defect.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Bohr's model works for helium."

❌ It works for He+ (helium ion, one electron) but not for neutral He (two electrons). The rule is: one electron only — H, He+, Li2+, Be3+.

⚠️ "Absorption spectrum = bright lines."

❌ Reversed. Absorption = dark lines on a bright background. Emission = bright lines on a dark background. Remember: absorption takes light away, so the lines are missing (dark).

⚠️ "Red light has a low wavelength because it's at the top."

❌ Red = 7000 Å = high wavelength, least deviated. Violet = 4000 Å = low wavelength, maximum deviation. The colour that bends most has the shortest wavelength.

⚠️ "Sunlight is monochromatic."

❌ Sunlight and bulb light are polychromatic — made of many wavelengths, which is why a prism splits them. Monochromatic means one wavelength only.

⚠️ "Bohr's model was rejected because it was useless."

❌ It was a huge step forward — it explained why atoms are stable and why line spectra exist, and its formulas still give the right answers for hydrogen. Its defects mean it is incomplete, not worthless. Quantum mechanics extends it rather than deleting it.

Short Questions

Q6. Write the four defects of Bohr's atomic model.

Answer:
  1. Applicable only to hydrogen and single-electron species (He+, Li2+, Be3+); it could not explain the spectra of multi-electron systems like He, Li, Be, B.
  2. It assumed circular orbits in a single plane, but the electron was later proved to move in three dimensional space.
  3. It gives the electron a definite distance and a definite velocity, which is against the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.
  4. It explains only the particle nature of the electron and not its wave nature (de Broglie's hypothesis).

Q7. Differentiate between a continuous spectrum and a line spectrum.

Answer: A continuous spectrum contains a continuous band of colours diffused into each other, with no marked boundary between them (e.g. sunlight through a prism, a rainbow). A line spectrum consists of certain distinct lines separated by dark space, produced by electrically or thermally excited atoms; the number of lines and their spacing depend on the element.

Q8. Why is a line spectrum called the "finger print" of an element?

Answer: Because each element emits light of specific wavelengths, so the number of lines and the distance between them depends upon the nature of the element. No two elements give the same pattern, so the line spectrum can be used to identify an element — exactly as a fingerprint identifies a person.

Q9. What information do line spectra give about electrons?

Answer: They show that electrons around the nucleus have a definite amount of energy and are arranged in definite energy levels. After absorbing energy an electron jumps from E1 to a higher level E2 and then returns, and the emitted energy difference E2 − E1 equals the energy absorbed.

Q10. Define monochromatic and polychromatic light.

Answer: Monochromatic light is composed of only one kind of ray or wavelength. Polychromatic light is composed of more than one type of ray — for example sunlight or bulb light, which splits into several colours when passed through a prism.

📝 Summary

5 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Bohr's four defects — "One · Flat · Certain · Particle": works for one electron only; assumes flat circular orbits; conflicts with Heisenberg's uncertainty principle; ignores the wave nature of the electron (de Broglie).
  2. Monochromatic = one wavelength. Polychromatic = many. A prism splits polychromatic light into a band of colours called a spectrum.
  3. Continuous spectrum — colours diffused into each other, VIBGYOR. Red = 7000 Å, least deviated; violet = 4000 Å, maximum deviation. A rainbow is the everyday example.
  4. Line spectrum — distinct lines separated by dark space from excited atoms; unique to each element, so it is the element's 'Finger Print' (sodium = two yellow lines).
  5. Absorption = dark lines on a bright background (electron jumps up). Emission = bright lines on a dark background (electron falls down).

Frequency & Wave Number of Radiation

Chapter 2 · Lesson 5 of 6 🧪 Chemistry ⏱ 14–16 min read Topic 2.2.1

🎯 Why Should You Care?

A sodium street lamp glows orange. A neon sign glows red. A mercury lamp glows blue-white. Why does each element produce its own colour — always the same one, every single time?

Because colour is frequency. And frequency comes straight out of Bohr's equations: when an electron falls from one orbit to another, the energy gap it releases fixes the frequency of the light — and therefore the colour you see.

This lesson turns the energy formula from Lesson 4 into two of the most useful equations in the chapter: one for frequency and one for wave number.

📖 Step 1: The Energy Released in a Jump

When an electron jumps from a higher energy orbit to a lower energy orbit, a definite amount of energy is emitted. Let E1 be the energy of the n1 orbit and E2 the energy of the n2 orbit. The emitted energy is written as:

ΔE = E2 − E1

Considering equation (viii) from the last lesson, the energies of the electron in the n1 and n2 orbits of the hydrogen atom are:

E1 = −me48εo2h2 ( 1n12 )   and   E2 = −me48εo2h2 ( 1n22 )
Derivation The energy-difference equation
Step 1 — subtract
ΔE = [ −me48εo2h2 ( 1n12 ) ] − [ −me48εo2h2 ( 1n22 ) ]
Step 2 — take the common factor out
ΔE = −me48εo2h2 ( 1n12 − 1n22 )   — — — — (x)
Step 3 — insert the numerical value of the constant bundle
ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 ( 1n12 − 1n22 ) joule/atom   — — — — (xi)
💡
What equation (xi) is for

This equation is used for determining the emission or absorption of energy when an electron jumps from one orbit to another. It is the single most useful numerical formula in this chapter.

🌊 Step 2: Frequency of the Emitted Radiation

If in equation (x) the energy difference ΔE appears as a photon, it would have a frequency υ, and thus according to Planck's quantum theory it should be ΔE = hυ.

Derivation The frequency equation
Step 1 — write equation (x) as ΔE = hυ
hυ = me48εo2h2 ( 1n12 − 1n22 )
Step 2 — divide both sides by h (h2 becomes h3)
υ = me48εo2h3 ( 1n12 − 1n22 ) Hertz   — — — — (xii)
🔍
Spot the only change

Compare (x) and (xii): the only difference is h2 becoming h3 in the denominator (and the sign convention). That is because you divided by h once. If you can remember the energy formula, you can rebuild the frequency formula in two seconds.

Self Assessment Using the Bohr model, determine the energy in joules of a photon produced when an electron in a hydrogen atom jumps from an orbit n = 5 to n = 2.
Step 1 — identify n1 and n2

The electron falls to n = 2 and from n = 5, so n1 = 2 (lower) and n2 = 5 (higher).

Step 2 — use equation (xi)
ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 ( 1(2)2 − 1(5)2 )
Step 3 — evaluate the bracket
14 − 125 = 0.25 − 0.04 = 0.21
Step 4 — multiply
ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 × 0.21 = 4.58 × 10−19 joule

📏 Step 3: Wave Number of Photons

📚 The link between frequency and wave number

The wave numbers of absorbed or emitted photons are calculated by the following relation:

υ = VC

Where V is the wave number and C is the velocity of light.

Derivation The wave number equation and the Rydberg constant
Step 1 — substitute υ = V̄C into equation (xii)
VC = me48εo2h3 ( 1n12 − 1n22 )
Step 2 — divide both sides by C
V = me48εo2h3C [ 1n12 − 1n22 ]
Step 3 — name the constant bundle
But me48εo2h3C = RH
Step 4 — the final form
V = RH [ 1n12 − 1n22 ]
📚 The Rydberg constant

Where RH is the Rydberg constant, and its value is 109678 cm−1. It may also be written as:

RH = 10967800 m−1   =   1.09678 × 107 m−1
📐
Match your units to RH

If you use RH = 109678 cm−1, your wave number comes out in cm−1. If you use 1.09678 × 107 m−1, it comes out in m−1. Read the question — it usually tells you which value to use.

Example 2.3 Calculate the wave number of a photon when the electron of a hydrogen atom jumps from the 4th orbit to the 2nd orbit (RH = 1.09678 × 107 m−1).
Step 1 — list the data

n1 = 2  ·  n2 = 4  ·  Z = 1  ·  V = ?

Step 2 — Bohr's equation for wave number
V = RH [ 1n12 − 1n22 ]
Step 3 — substitute
V = (1.09678 × 107) [ 1(2)2 − 1(4)2 ]
Step 4 — evaluate the bracket
0.25 − 0.0625 = 0.187 (to 3 s.f.)
Step 5 — multiply
V = (1.09678 × 107) × 0.187 = 2.051 × 106 m−1
Self Assessment What is the wave number of a photon produced when an electron falls from the n = 5 level to the n = 3 level in a hydrogen atom?
Step 1 — list the data

n1 = 3 (lower)  ·  n2 = 5 (higher)  ·  RH = 1.09678 × 107 m−1

Step 2 — substitute
V = (1.09678 × 107) [ 1(3)2 − 1(5)2 ]
Step 3 — evaluate the bracket
19 − 125 = 0.1111 − 0.04 = 0.0711
Step 4 — multiply
V = (1.09678 × 107) × 0.0711 = 7.80 × 105 m−1

✅
The one rule that prevents most mistakes

Always put the smaller n as n1 and the larger n as n2. Then (1/n12 − 1/n22) is always positive, and your energy, frequency and wave number all come out positive — which is what a physical answer must be.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Falling down stairs 🪜 — Drop from step 5 to step 2 and you release a fixed amount of energy. Drop from 5 to 3 and you release less. Different drops, different energies, different colours.
  2. Street lamps 🟠 — Sodium lamps always glow the same orange because the same electron jump happens in every sodium atom, releasing exactly the same frequency.
  3. Wave number = waves per metre 📏 — Frequency counts waves per second; wave number counts waves per metre. Same wave, two ways to count it. That is why they differ only by C.
  4. The gap shrinks as you go out 📉 — Since energy goes as 1/n2, the gap between n = 1 and 2 is huge, while between n = 5 and 6 it is tiny. High orbits are crowded close together.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "n1 is where the electron starts."

⚠️ In these formulas n1 is the lower orbit and n2 is the higher orbit — regardless of which way the electron travels. For a jump from 5 → 2, n1 = 2 and n2 = 5. Getting this backwards gives a negative answer.

⚠️ "Wave number is the same as wavelength."

❌ They are reciprocals. Wave number (V) = 1/λ, with units m−1 or cm−1. A large wave number means a short wavelength.

⚠️ "RH = 109678 always."

⚠️ That value is in cm−1. In metres it is 1.09678 × 107 m−1. Using the cm value and reporting m−1 is out by a factor of 100.

⚠️ "υ (frequency) and V (wave number) are the same symbol."

⚠️ They look similar in print, which is exactly why students mix them up. Frequency υ is in Hertz. Wave number carries a bar over it and is in m−1. They are linked by υ = VC.

Numerical & Short Questions

Q5. Calculate the energy emitted when an electron in a hydrogen atom jumps from n = 3 to n = 1.

Answer: n1 = 1, n2 = 3.
ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 (1/1 − 1/9) = 2.18 × 10−18 × 0.8889 = 1.94 × 10−18 joule.

Q6. Find the wave number of the photon emitted when an electron falls from n = 3 to n = 2 in hydrogen (RH = 1.09678 × 107 m−1).

Answer: n1 = 2, n2 = 3.
V̄ = 1.09678 × 107 (1/4 − 1/9) = 1.09678 × 107 × 0.1389 = 1.52 × 106 m−1.

Q7. Derive the expression for the frequency of radiation emitted when an electron jumps between two orbits.

Answer: Starting from E = (−me4/8εo2h2)(1/n2), write E1 and E2 for the two orbits and subtract to get ΔE = (−me4/8εo2h2)(1/n12 − 1/n22). Since the energy difference appears as a photon, ΔE = hυ. Dividing both sides by h gives υ = (me4/8εo2h3)(1/n12 − 1/n22) Hertz.

Q8. How is the Rydberg constant obtained from Bohr's equations?

Answer: Substituting υ = VC into the frequency equation and dividing by C gives V̄ = (me4/8εo2h3C)(1/n12 − 1/n22). The constant bundle me4/8εo2h3C is defined as RH, the Rydberg constant = 109678 cm−1 = 1.09678 × 107 m−1.

📝 Summary

5 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Energy of a jump: ΔE = 2.18 × 10−18 (1/n12 − 1/n22) J/atom — used for both emission and absorption.
  2. Since ΔE = hυ, the frequency is υ = (me4/8εo2h3)(1/n12 − 1/n22) Hertz — note h2 became h3.
  3. Wave number and frequency are linked by υ = VC.
  4. This gives V̄ = RH(1/n12 − 1/n22), where RH = 109678 cm−1 = 1.09678 × 107 m−1.
  5. Always take n1 = the lower orbit so the bracket stays positive. Worked answer to remember: 4 → 2 in hydrogen gives 2.051 × 106 m−1.

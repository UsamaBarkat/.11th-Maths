Properties of Addition & Scalar Multiplication

Unit 3 · Lesson 4 of 8 🔢 Mathematics ⏱ 14–16 min read Sections 3.3 – 3.4

🎯 Why Should You Care?

In the last three lessons you added vectors, subtracted them and scaled them — and you almost certainly rewrote \(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\) as \(\vec{B}+\vec{A}\) somewhere along the way without stopping to ask whether you were allowed to.

You were. But why you were is the whole content of this lesson. Vectors are not numbers: a vector carries a direction as well as a size, and there is no reason on the face of it that moving 5 km east then 3 km north should land you in the same place as moving 3 km north then 5 km east. It does — and the proof is a parallelogram.

Sections 3.3 and 3.4 collect the eight rules that make vector algebra safe: four for addition (commutative, associative, an identity \(\vec{0}\), an inverse \(-\vec{A}\)) and four for scalar multiplication (commutative, associative and two distributive laws). Every rearrangement you will ever make in the dot-product and cross-product lessons ahead is licensed by one of these eight. Learn them once here and you never have to think about them again.

➕ Commutative Law for Vector Addition

📚 Statement

"The sum of two vectors remains the same irrespective of their order."

Let \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) be two vectors. Then by the commutative law for vector addition:

\[\vec{A}+\vec{B}=\vec{B}+\vec{A}\]

The construction

Consider two vectors \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\), which represent two adjacent sides of a parallelogram OPQR as shown in Fig 3.25. Because opposite sides of a parallelogram are equal and parallel, the same two vectors appear twice each — \(\vec{B}\) along both \(\overrightarrow{OP}\) and \(\overrightarrow{RQ}\), and \(\vec{A}\) along both \(\overrightarrow{OR}\) and \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\).

Fig 3.25 — Parallelogram OPQR. Travelling O → P → Q adds B⃗ then A⃗; travelling O → R → Q adds A⃗ then B⃗. Both routes finish at Q, so both describe the same resultant R⃗ (drawn in the accent colour).

The proof — two triangles, one diagonal

The diagonal \(\overrightarrow{OQ}=\vec{R}\) closes both triangles, so we can apply the triangle law of vector addition twice over.

In \(\triangle OPQ\), by the triangle law of vector addition:

\[\vec{R}=\vec{B}+\vec{A}\qquad \text{...(i)}\]

In \(\triangle OQR\), by the triangle law of vector addition:

\[\vec{R}=\vec{A}+\vec{B}\qquad \text{...(ii)}\]

From (i) and (ii), we get:

\[\vec{A}+\vec{B}=\vec{B}+\vec{A}\]
🔑
Why the parallelogram does all the work

The two triangles are not two different calculations — they are two different readings of the same diagonal. Because \(\vec{R}\) is one arrow, and because two things equal to the same thing are equal to each other, the order of the sum cannot matter. That single diagonal is the entire proof.

🔗 Associative Law for Vector Addition

📚 Statement

"The sum of three vectors remains the same irrespective of their order or the grouping in which they are arranged."

Let \(\vec{A}\), \(\vec{B}\) and \(\vec{C}\) be three vectors. Then by the associative law of vector addition:

\[\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\]

The construction

Consider three vectors \(\vec{A}\), \(\vec{B}\), \(\vec{C}\) and their resultant vector \(\vec{R}\) represented by the line segments \(\overrightarrow{OP}\), \(\overrightarrow{PQ}\), \(\overrightarrow{QR}\) and \(\overrightarrow{OR}\) respectively, as shown in Fig 3.26.

We apply the triangle law of vector addition to obtain the two groupings \(\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)\) and \(\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)\) — these are the two diagonals of the figure.

Fig 3.26 — The two diagonals are the two groupings. A⃗ + B⃗ is the diagonal O → Q; B⃗ + C⃗ is the diagonal P → R. Whichever pair you add first, the journey still ends at R, so the resultant R⃗ (accent colour) is the same.

The proof — two triangles, one base

Now in \(\triangle OPR\):

\[\overrightarrow{OR}=\overrightarrow{OP}+\overrightarrow{PR}\]

i.e.

\[\vec{R}=\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)\qquad \text{...(i)}\]

And in \(\triangle OQR\):

\[\overrightarrow{OR}=\overrightarrow{OQ}+\overrightarrow{QR}\]

i.e.

\[\vec{R}=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\qquad \text{...(ii)}\]

From (i) and (ii) we get:

\[\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\]
Example \(\vec{A}=5\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\), \(\vec{B}=\hat{i}+5\hat{j}-7\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{C}=\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\) are three vectors. Verify the associative law of vector addition.
Verification — the law to be checked
\[\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\]
Step 1 — evaluate the left-hand side
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{L.H.S}&=\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)\\[2pt] &=\left(5\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)+\left[\left(\hat{i}+5\hat{j}-7\hat{k}\right)+\left(\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)\right]\\[2pt] &=\left(5\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)+\left(2\hat{i}+6\hat{j}-8\hat{k}\right)\\[2pt] &=(5+2)\hat{i}+(1+6)\hat{j}+(-1-8)\hat{k}\\[2pt] &=7\hat{i}+7\hat{j}-9\hat{k} \end{aligned} \]
Step 2 — evaluate the right-hand side
\[ \begin{aligned} \text{R.H.S}&=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\\[2pt] &=\left[\left(5\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)+\left(\hat{i}+5\hat{j}-7\hat{k}\right)\right]+\left(\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)\\[2pt] &=\left(6\hat{i}+6\hat{j}-8\hat{k}\right)+\left(\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)\\[2pt] &=(6+1)\hat{i}+(6+1)\hat{j}+(-8-1)\hat{k}\\[2pt] &=7\hat{i}+7\hat{j}-9\hat{k} \end{aligned} \]
Step 3 — compare

Since \(\text{L.H.S}=\text{R.H.S}\), therefore \(\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\).

AnswerBoth sides equal \(7\hat{i}+7\hat{j}-9\hat{k}\) — hence verified.

0️⃣ The Zero Vector Is the Identity for Vector Addition

📚 Statement

Let \(V\) be the set of all vectors in space, containing the vector \(\vec{0}\) which satisfies the property:

\(\vec{0}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}+\vec{0}=\vec{v}\), for any vector \(\vec{v}\in V\).

Therefore, \(\vec{0}\) is the identity for vector addition.

Proof

Let \(\vec{v}=v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k}\).

Then there exists a vector \(\vec{0}=0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\), such that:

\[ \vec{v}+\vec{0}=\left(v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k}\right)+\left(0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\right)=v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k} \]

So,

\[\vec{v}+\vec{0}=\vec{v}\]

and similarly we can prove that \(\vec{0}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}\).

Hence, \(\vec{0}\) is the additive identity, or identity for vector addition.

🧭
\(\vec{0}\) is a vector, not the number 0

The zero vector \(\vec{0}=0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\) has magnitude zero and no defined direction. It is the displacement of standing perfectly still: adding it to any journey changes nothing. Writing \(\vec{v}+0\) with a plain scalar zero is not a legal expression — you cannot add a number to a vector.

↔️ −A⃗ Is the Additive Inverse of A⃗

📚 Statement

Let \(\vec{A}\) be a vector. The additive inverse \(-\vec{A}\), which is also called the negative vector of \(\vec{A}\), is a vector which when added to \(\vec{A}\) gives the additive identity, i.e.

\[\vec{A}+\left(-\vec{A}\right)=\vec{0}\]

Proof

Let \(\vec{A}=a_1\hat{i}+a_2\hat{j}+a_3\hat{k}\) be a vector in space.

Then \(-\vec{A}=-a_1\hat{i}-a_2\hat{j}-a_3\hat{k}\).

Now,

\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{A}+\left(-\vec{A}\right)&=a_1\hat{i}+a_2\hat{j}+a_3\hat{k}+\left(-a_1\hat{i}-a_2\hat{j}-a_3\hat{k}\right)\\[2pt] &=a_1\hat{i}+a_2\hat{j}+a_3\hat{k}-a_1\hat{i}-a_2\hat{j}-a_3\hat{k}\;=\;\vec{0} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(-\vec{A}\) is the additive inverse of \(\vec{A}\).

Example If \(\vec{A}=4\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{B}=-4\hat{i}-\hat{j}+\hat{k}\), then show that \(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) are additive inverses of each other.
Solution — add the two vectors component by component
\[ \begin{aligned} \vec{A}+\vec{B}&=\left(4\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}\right)+\left(-4\hat{i}-\hat{j}+\hat{k}\right)\\[2pt] &=(4-4)\hat{i}+(1-1)\hat{j}+(-1+1)\hat{k}\;=\;\vec{0} \end{aligned} \]
Conclusion

Since \(\vec{A}+\vec{B}=\vec{0}\), the sum is the additive identity.

Answer\(\vec{A}\) and \(\vec{B}\) are additive inverses of each other. Hence shown.

✖️ Scalar Multiplication: Commutative and Associative Laws

Section 3.4 turns from adding vectors to stretching them. The book asks us to state and verify three properties of scalar multiplication:

#LawStatement
(i)Commutative law for scalar multiplication\(m\!\left(\vec{a}\right)=\left(\vec{a}\right)m\)
(ii)Associative law for scalar multiplication\(m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)=(mn)\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\)
(iii)Distributive laws for scalar multiplication\(m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\)  and  \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\)

Throughout section 3.4 the book writes a vector in component (row) form, \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\), because every one of these proofs is just the matching property of ordinary real numbers applied to each component in turn.

(i) Commutative law for scalar multiplication

Let \(\vec{a}\) be a vector and \(m\) be any scalar such that \(m\in\mathbb{R}\). Then

\[m\!\left(\vec{a}\right)=\left(\vec{a}\right)m\]

is called the commutative law for scalar multiplication.

✍️ Proof

Let \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\) be a vector, then

\[ \begin{aligned} m\!\left(\vec{a}\right)&=m[x,\,y,\,z]=[mx,\,my,\,mz]\\[2pt] \text{and}\quad [mx,\,my,\,mz]&=[xm,\,ym,\,zm]\\[2pt] &=[x,\,y,\,z]\,m=\left(\vec{a}\right)m \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(m\!\left(\vec{a}\right)=\left(\vec{a}\right)m\). Hence proved.

(ii) Associative law for scalar multiplication

Let \(\vec{a}\) be a vector and \(m,\,n\) be scalars such that \(m,\,n\in\mathbb{R}\). Then

\[m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)=(mn)\vec{a}\]

is called the associative law for scalar multiplication.

✍️ Proof

Let \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\) be a vector, then

\[ \begin{aligned} n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)&=n[x,\,y,\,z]=[nx,\,ny,\,nz]\\[2pt] \text{Now}\quad m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)&=m[nx,\,ny,\,nz]\\[2pt] &=[m(nx),\,m(ny),\,m(nz)]\\[2pt] &=(mn)[x,\,y,\,z]\\[2pt] &=(mn)\vec{a} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)=(mn)\vec{a}\). Hence proved.

💡
Do You Know? — where the proof actually happens

Look closely at the step \([m(nx),\,m(ny),\,m(nz)]=[(mn)x,\,(mn)y,\,(mn)z]\). Nothing about vectors is used there — it is the associative law of multiplication for real numbers, applied three times. Every proof in section 3.4 works this way: the vector property is inherited, component by component, from the arithmetic you already trust.

📤 Distributive Laws for Scalar Multiplication

Let \(\vec{a}\) and \(\vec{b}\) be two vectors in space and \(m,\,n\in\mathbb{R}\) be scalars. Then

\[\text{(i)}\quad m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\qquad\qquad \text{(ii)}\quad (m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\]

are called the distributive laws for scalar multiplication. Note that they distribute over different sums: the first spreads one scalar across a sum of vectors, the second spreads one vector across a sum of scalars.

(i) \(m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\)

✍️ Proof

Let \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\) and \(\vec{b}=[u,\,v,\,w]\) be two vectors in space and \(m\in\mathbb{R}\) be a scalar. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)&=m\left\{[x,\,y,\,z]+[u,\,v,\,w]\right\}=m[x+u,\;y+v,\;z+w]\\[2pt] &=[mx+mu,\;my+mv,\;mz+mw]\\[2pt] &=[mx,\,my,\,mz]+[mu,\,mv,\,mw]\\[2pt] &=m[x,\,y,\,z]+m[u,\,v,\,w]=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \(m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\). Hence proved.

(ii) \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\)

✍️ Proof

Let \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\) be a vector in space and \(m,\,n\in\mathbb{R}\) be scalars. Then

\[ \begin{aligned} (m+n)\vec{a}&=(m+n)[x,\,y,\,z]=[(m+n)x,\;(m+n)y,\;(m+n)z]\\[2pt] &=[(mx+nx),\;(my+ny),\;(mz+nz)]\\[2pt] &=[mx,\,my,\,mz]+[nx,\,ny,\,nz]\\[2pt] &=m[x,\,y,\,z]+n[x,\,y,\,z]\\[2pt] &=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a} \end{aligned} \]

Thus, \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\). Hence proved.

🔍
Spot the difference between the two

In law (i) the plus sign inside the bracket joins two vectors and \(m\) is a single scalar. In law (ii) the plus sign inside the bracket joins two scalars and \(\vec{a}\) is a single vector. They look almost identical on the page and are proved almost identically — but they are two separate statements, which is why the book numbers them separately.

⚡
Two proof techniques, that is all

Properties 1 and 2 are proved geometrically — draw the figure, apply the triangle law twice, read the same arrow two ways. Properties 3 to 8 are proved algebraically — write the vector in components and let ordinary real-number arithmetic do the work. If you can reproduce one proof of each kind, you can reproduce all eight.

💡 Real-Life Way to Think About It

  1. Two streets to the same corner 🚶 — Walk one block east then one block north, or one block north then one block east. Different pavements, identical corner. That corner is the diagonal of Fig 3.25, and it is why addition is commutative.
  2. Packing a three-leg trip 🧳 — Home → airport → hotel → office. You can think of it as "the trip to the hotel, then on to the office", or "home to the airport, then the rest of the journey". The grouping is a story you tell yourself; the office does not move. That is the associative law.
  3. Standing still ⏸️ — \(\vec{0}\) is the move that is not a move. Insert it anywhere in a journey and the destination is unchanged. That is what "identity" means.
  4. Retracing your steps ↩️ — \(-\vec{A}\) is the same walk taken backwards: same distance, opposite direction. Do both and you are back where you started, which is exactly \(\vec{A}+\left(-\vec{A}\right)=\vec{0}\).
  5. Doubling a recipe 🍲 — Doubling "flour plus sugar" is the same as doubling the flour and doubling the sugar. Distributing the scalar across the sum is a thing you already do without thinking; law 7 says vectors behave the same way.
  6. Three laps plus two laps 🏃 — Running a track \(3+2\) times is running it 3 times and then 2 more. Same track, added counts — that is law 8, and note it is the scalars being added, not the laps.

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ "Addition is commutative, so subtraction must be too."

❌ It is not. \(\vec{A}-\vec{B}\) and \(\vec{B}-\vec{A}\) have the same magnitude but opposite directions — they are additive inverses of each other, not equals. Only addition was proved commutative in 3.3.1, and the proof used a parallelogram that has no equivalent for subtraction.

⚠️ "\(\vec{0}\) and \(0\) are the same thing."

❌ \(\vec{0}=0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\) is a vector with zero magnitude and no defined direction. \(0\) is a scalar. \(\vec{A}+\left(-\vec{A}\right)=\vec{0}\) is correct; writing \(=0\) is not, because the two sides would not even be the same kind of object.

⚠️ "\(-\vec{A}\) is a shorter vector, or points downwards."

❌ The negative vector has exactly the same magnitude as \(\vec{A}\). Only the direction reverses — it points along the same line, the opposite way. "Negative" here means reversed, never smaller.

⚠️ "\(m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+\vec{b}\)."

❌ The scalar must reach every term inside the bracket. Law 7 gives \(m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\). Scaling only the first vector changes the resultant's direction as well as its length — it is not a small slip, it is a different answer.

⚠️ "The associative law for scalar multiplication is the same as the one for addition."

❌ They are different statements about different operations. \(\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\) regroups three vectors being added (proved with Fig 3.26). \(m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)=(mn)\vec{a}\) regroups two scalars stretching one vector (proved in components). Same word, different law.

Short Questions

Q6. State the commutative and associative laws for vector addition, and name the figure used to prove each.

Answer: The commutative law states that the sum of two vectors remains the same irrespective of their order: \(\vec{A}+\vec{B}=\vec{B}+\vec{A}\). It is proved from the parallelogram OPQR of Fig 3.25, by applying the triangle law in \(\triangle OPQ\) and again in \(\triangle OQR\) — both readings give the same diagonal \(\vec{R}\). The associative law states that the sum of three vectors remains the same irrespective of their order or grouping: \(\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\). It is proved from Fig 3.26, by applying the triangle law in \(\triangle OPR\) and again in \(\triangle OQR\) — both give the same base \(\overrightarrow{OR}=\vec{R}\).

Q7. Prove, using components, that \(\vec{0}\) is the identity for vector addition.

Answer: Let \(\vec{v}=v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k}\). There exists a vector \(\vec{0}=0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\) such that \(\vec{v}+\vec{0}=\left(v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k}\right)+\left(0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\right)=v_1\hat{i}+v_2\hat{j}+v_3\hat{k}=\vec{v}\). Similarly \(\vec{0}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}\). Since \(\vec{0}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}+\vec{0}=\vec{v}\) for every \(\vec{v}\), the vector \(\vec{0}\) is the additive identity for vector addition.

Q8. Verify the associative law of vector addition for \(\vec{A}=2\hat{i}-\hat{j}+3\hat{k}\), \(\vec{B}=\hat{i}+4\hat{j}-\hat{k}\) and \(\vec{C}=-3\hat{i}+\hat{j}+2\hat{k}\).

Answer: L.H.S: \(\vec{B}+\vec{C}=(1-3)\hat{i}+(4+1)\hat{j}+(-1+2)\hat{k}=-2\hat{i}+5\hat{j}+\hat{k}\), so \(\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=(2-2)\hat{i}+(-1+5)\hat{j}+(3+1)\hat{k}=4\hat{j}+4\hat{k}\). R.H.S: \(\vec{A}+\vec{B}=(2+1)\hat{i}+(-1+4)\hat{j}+(3-1)\hat{k}=3\hat{i}+3\hat{j}+2\hat{k}\), so \(\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}=(3-3)\hat{i}+(3+1)\hat{j}+(2+2)\hat{k}=4\hat{j}+4\hat{k}\). Since L.H.S = R.H.S = \(4\hat{j}+4\hat{k}\), the associative law is verified.

Q9. Let \(\vec{a}=[2,\,-1,\,4]\), \(m=3\) and \(n=-2\). Verify that \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\).

Answer: L.H.S: \((m+n)\vec{a}=\left(3+(-2)\right)[2,\,-1,\,4]=1\cdot[2,\,-1,\,4]=[2,\,-1,\,4]\). R.H.S: \(m\vec{a}=3[2,\,-1,\,4]=[6,\,-3,\,12]\) and \(n\vec{a}=-2[2,\,-1,\,4]=[-4,\,2,\,-8]\), so \(m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}=[6-4,\;-3+2,\;12-8]=[2,\,-1,\,4]\). Since L.H.S = R.H.S = \([2,\,-1,\,4]\), the distributive law \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\) is verified.

📝 Summary

7 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. Commutative law for vector addition — \(\vec{A}+\vec{B}=\vec{B}+\vec{A}\). Proved from parallelogram OPQR (Fig 3.25) by applying the triangle law in \(\triangle OPQ\) and \(\triangle OQR\): one diagonal, read two ways.
  2. Associative law for vector addition — \(\vec{A}+\left(\vec{B}+\vec{C}\right)=\left(\vec{A}+\vec{B}\right)+\vec{C}\). Proved from Fig 3.26 by applying the triangle law in \(\triangle OPR\) and \(\triangle OQR\): one base \(\overrightarrow{OR}\), two groupings.
  3. \(\vec{0}\) is the additive identity — \(\vec{0}+\vec{v}=\vec{v}+\vec{0}=\vec{v}\) for every \(\vec{v}\). It is a vector of zero magnitude, \(0\hat{i}+0\hat{j}+0\hat{k}\), not the scalar \(0\).
  4. \(-\vec{A}\) is the additive inverse — \(\vec{A}+\left(-\vec{A}\right)=\vec{0}\). The negative vector has the same magnitude as \(\vec{A}\) and the opposite direction.
  5. Scalar multiplication is commutative and associative — \(m\!\left(\vec{a}\right)=\left(\vec{a}\right)m\) and \(m\!\left(n\!\left(\vec{a}\right)\right)=(mn)\vec{a}\), both proved by writing \(\vec{a}=[x,\,y,\,z]\) and using real-number arithmetic on each component.
  6. Two distributive laws — \(m\!\left(\vec{a}+\vec{b}\right)=m\vec{a}+m\vec{b}\) spreads one scalar over a sum of vectors; \((m+n)\vec{a}=m\vec{a}+n\vec{a}\) spreads one vector over a sum of scalars. They are separate statements.
  7. Two proof techniques cover all eight properties — the two addition laws are geometric (triangle law applied twice to one figure); the remaining six are algebraic (component form, then ordinary arithmetic).

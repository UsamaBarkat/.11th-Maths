Introduction to Complex Numbers

Unit 1 · Lesson 1 of 3 🔢 Mathematics ⏱ 15–20 min read

🎯 The Problem That Started It All

Try to solve this equation: x² = −1

In Class 9 and 10, you would say "no solution" — because squaring any real number (positive or negative) always gives a positive result. You can never square something and get −1.

But mathematicians refused to give up. They asked: "What if we just invent a new number that solves it?" That's exactly what they did — and the result changed mathematics forever.

📖 Core Concept: The Imaginary Unit and Complex Numbers

📚 The Imaginary Unit

Mathematicians defined a new number called i (imaginary unit) where:

\[ i = \sqrt{-1} \quad \Longrightarrow \quad i^2 = -1 \]

✅ Simple Version

i is a number invented to solve x² = −1. It doesn't exist on the real number line — it's in a completely new dimension.

What is a Complex Number?

📚 Definition

A complex number is any number of the form:

\[ z = a + bi \]

where a is the real part, b is the imaginary part, and i = √(−1).

✅ Quick Test

Re(z) = a (real part)  |  Im(z) = b (imaginary part — the number in front of i, not i itself)

Powers of i — The Cycle of 4

The powers of i repeat in a cycle of 4. Memorise this:

PowerValueHow?
i¹iJust i
i²−1By definition
i³−ii² × i = (−1)(i) = −i
i⁴1i² × i² = (−1)(−1) = 1
i⁵iBack to start! (cycle repeats)
✅ Shortcut for High Powers

For any power iⁿ: divide n by 4, look at the remainder.

Remainder 0 → 1  |  Remainder 1 → i  |  Remainder 2 → −1  |  Remainder 3 → −i

Example: i¹⁰ → 10 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 2 → i¹⁰ = −1

The Argand Plane (Complex Plane)

Complex numbers can be plotted on a graph called the Argand plane:

  • Horizontal axis = Real axis (for part a)
  • Vertical axis = Imaginary axis (for part b)
  • Complex number z = a + bi → plot point (a, b)
    Imaginary axis (b)
          ↑
    7i    |        • (−5 + 7i)
          |
    4i    |              • (3 + 4i)
          |
─────────┼──────────────────→ Real axis (a)
   −5 −4 │ 0   1   2   3   4
          |
   −3i   |              • (4 − 3i)
          |

Every complex number is a unique point on this plane. The distance from the origin (0,0) to the point (a, b) is called the modulus of z.

💡 The 4 Operations on Complex Numbers

➕ Addition

Rule: Add real parts together, add imaginary parts together.

\[ (a + bi) + (c + di) = (a+c) + (b+d)i \]

Example: (3 + 4i) + (2 + 5i) = (3+2) + (4+5)i = 5 + 9i

➖ Subtraction

Rule: Subtract real parts, subtract imaginary parts.

\[ (a + bi) - (c + di) = (a-c) + (b-d)i \]

Example: (5 + 7i) − (2 + 3i) = (5−2) + (7−3)i = 3 + 4i

✖️ Multiplication (FOIL method)

Rule: Use FOIL (like expanding brackets), then replace i² with −1.

Example: (2 + 3i)(4 + 5i)

= 8 + 10i + 12i + 15i² = 8 + 22i + 15(−1) = (8−15) + 22i = −7 + 22i

➗ Division (Conjugate method)

What is a conjugate? The conjugate of (a + bi) is (a − bi). Just flip the sign of b.

Rule: Multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. This makes the denominator a real number.

Why it works: \((a + bi)(a - bi) = a^2 + b^2\) (always real!)

Example: (4 + 3i) ÷ (1 + 2i)

= \(\dfrac{(4+3i)(1-2i)}{(1+2i)(1-2i)} = \dfrac{4-8i+3i-6i^2}{1+4} = \dfrac{4-5i+6}{5} = \dfrac{10-5i}{5} =\) 2 − i

⚠️ Common Confusions

⚠️ Confusion #1: "The imaginary part of 3 − 4i is 4i, not −4"

❌ Wrong. The imaginary part is just the coefficient of i — which is −4, not 4i. Im(3 − 4i) = −4. This is a real number.

⚠️ Confusion #2: "i² = √(−1) × √(−1) = √(−1 × −1) = √1 = 1"

❌ Wrong! The rule √a × √b = √(ab) only works when a and b are positive. The correct answer is i² = −1 by definition. Don't apply real-number square root rules to imaginary numbers.

⚠️ Confusion #3: "Imaginary means fake or made up"

✅ The name is confusing, but imaginary numbers are as real as negative numbers (which were also once considered "fake"). They're used in electrical engineering, quantum physics, and signal processing every day.

⚠️ Confusion #4: Forgetting to replace i² with −1 during multiplication

❌ The most common mistake! When you get a term with i², always replace it with −1 immediately. 15i² = 15(−1) = −15, not 15.

Q7. Why do we multiply by the conjugate when dividing complex numbers?

When we multiply a complex number (a + bi) by its conjugate (a − bi), we get:
(a + bi)(a − bi) = a² + b² — a pure real number (no i).
This turns the denominator into a real number, which makes division straightforward.

📝 Summary

3 Key Points from This Lesson

  1. i = √(−1), so i² = −1, i³ = −i, i⁴ = 1 — the cycle repeats every 4 powers.
  2. A complex number z = a + bi has a real part a and imaginary part b. It can be plotted as point (a, b) on the Argand plane.
  3. For division, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator to eliminate i from the bottom.

1.1  What Physics Is, and Why We Measure

Look around you. A bird glides without flapping, a car turns stored petrol into motion, the sky is blue, your phone buzzes with a message from far away. Every one of these follows the same set of rules — the rules of physics.

📖 Definition — Physics

Physics is the branch of science that studies matter, energy, space and time, and how they interact with one another. Its goal is to find a small set of laws (written as mathematical equations) that can explain what we see and predict what will happen next.

Physics is huge, so we split it into sub-fields. You don't need to memorise these now — just get a feel for the map of the subject:

BranchWhat it studies
Classical mechanicsMotion of ordinary objects and the forces on them (Newton's laws, momentum, energy).
ElectromagnetismElectric and magnetic fields, charges, currents and light as an electromagnetic wave.
ThermodynamicsHeat, temperature and energy transfer.
OpticsThe behaviour of light — reflection, refraction, diffraction.
Quantum mechanicsThe strange rules that govern atoms and sub-atomic particles.
RelativityObjects moving very fast or in strong gravity; how space and time bend.

Physics in science, technology and society

Physics is not just theory. It gives engineers the laws they use to build bridges, power grids and microchips; it gives doctors X-rays, MRI and laser surgery; and it trains anyone who studies it to reason carefully from evidence. That habit — measure, calculate, check — is exactly what this unit is about.

💡 The big idea of this unit

Physics is built on measurement. A law is only trusted if its predictions match measured numbers. So the very first skill in physics is knowing how to measure a quantity, state its unit, and honestly report how sure you are of it.

1.2  Physical Quantities and the SI Units

📖 Definition — Physical quantity

A physical quantity is anything in nature that can be measured, such as length, mass, time or temperature. Measuring it always gives two parts: a number and a unit. Writing "5" is meaningless; writing "5 metres" tells us how much.

A unit is a fixed, agreed-upon amount of a quantity that we compare everything else against. If everyone used their own foot to measure length, no two answers would agree — so the world adopted one shared system: the SI system (Système International d'Unités, French for "International System of Units").

The SI system starts from seven base quantities. They are called "base" because they are chosen as the starting point — everything else is built from them.

1.2.2  Derived units

📖 Definition — Derived unit

A derived unit is made by multiplying or dividing base units together. For example, speed is length ÷ time, so its unit is the metre per second, m/s.

The unit of force, the newton ($\mathrm{N}$), is a derived unit: since force = mass × acceleration, $1\ \mathrm{N} = 1\ \mathrm{kg\cdot m/s^2}$. A few more:

Two special quantities — plane angle and solid angle — are pure ratios, so they have a unit but no dimensions (more on dimensions soon). They are called supplementary units.

1.2.4  Conventions and prefixes

To keep everyone consistent, SI has rules for writing units:

  • Unit symbols are written in lower case (m, s, kg) — unless the unit is named after a scientist, then the symbol is capital (N for Newton, A for Ampère, K for Kelvin).
  • Use a unit that fits the size of the thing measured: metres for a room, kilometres for a road.

For very large or very small amounts we attach a prefix, a word-part that multiplies the unit by a power of ten:

PrefixSymbolMultiplies by

megaM$10^{6}$

So $1\ \mathrm{km} = 10^{3}\ \mathrm{m}$ and $1\ \mathrm{ms} = 10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s}$.

1.3  How We Actually Measure Things

Knowing the unit is half the job; you also need an instrument. Here are the standard tools for each base quantity you will meet this year.

🔎 The ticker timer & the C.R.O.

A ticker timer makes dots on a paper tape at equal time intervals (e.g. every $\tfrac{1}{50}\,\mathrm{s}$). Dots close together mean slow motion; dots far apart mean fast motion — so the tape is a record of speed. A C.R.O. plots voltage (vertical) against time (horizontal), letting you literally see and measure an electrical signal.

1.4  Dimensions & Dimensional Analysis

📖 Definition — Dimension

The dimension of a quantity tells you which base quantities it is built from, and to what power. We write the base dimensions in square brackets: length $[L]$, mass $[M]$, time $[T]$, electric current $[A]$ and temperature $[K]$.

Think of dimensions as the "recipe" of a quantity, ignoring the actual numbers. Speed is length divided by time, so its dimension is

$$\text{speed}=\frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}=\frac{[L]}{[T]}=[M^0\,L\,T^{-1}]$$
Reading this
$M^0$
mass appears to the power 0 — i.e. speed does not depend on mass.
$L$
length appears to the power +1.
$T^{-1}$
time appears to the power −1 (it is "per second").

Here are the dimensions of quantities you will use all year. Notice that different quantities can share the same dimensions (work and energy, for instance):

QuantityBuilt fromDimensional formula
Arealength × breadth$[L^2]$
Densitymass ÷ volume$[M\,L^{-3}]$
Velocitydisplacement ÷ time$[L\,T^{-1}]$
Accelerationvelocity ÷ time$[L\,T^{-2}]$
Forcemass × acceleration$[M\,L\,T^{-2}]$
Momentummass × velocity$[M\,L\,T^{-1}]$
Work / Energyforce × displacement$[M\,L^2\,T^{-2}]$
Powerwork ÷ time$[M\,L^2\,T^{-3}]$
Pressureforce ÷ area$[M\,L^{-1}\,T^{-2}]$
Electric chargecurrent × time$[A\,T]$

1.4.1  Use #1 — Checking an equation (homogeneity)

A correct physics equation must have the same dimensions on both sides — you cannot equate metres to seconds. This is called the principle of homogeneity, and it is a quick way to catch a wrong formula.

Worked example 1.1Show that the impulse equation $Ft = m\,v_f - m\,v_i = m\,\Delta v$ is dimensionally correct.
Step 1 — write each side in dimensions

Left side $Ft$: force × time $=[M\,L\,T^{-2}]\,[T]=[M\,L\,T^{-1}]$.

Right side $m\,\Delta v$: mass × velocity $=[M]\,[L\,T^{-1}]=[M\,L\,T^{-1}]$.

Step 2 — compare

Both sides equal $[M\,L\,T^{-1}]$ (the dimensions of momentum), so the equation is homogeneous and therefore dimensionally correct. ✓

AnswerBoth sides have dimension $[M\,L\,T^{-1}]$ → the equation is dimensionally consistent.

1.4.2  Use #2 — Deriving a formula

If we can guess which quantities a result depends on, dimensions can often reconstruct the formula (up to a plain number). Take the time period $T$ of a simple pendulum. It is reasonable to assume $T$ depends on the bob's mass $m$, the string length $L$, and gravity $g$ (with $[g]=[L\,T^{-2}]$):

$$T = k\,m^{x}\,L^{y}\,g^{z}$$
where
$k$
a dimensionless number (dimensions can't find it).
$x,y,z$
unknown powers we will solve for.

Writing both sides in dimensions: $[T] = [M]^{x}\,[L]^{y}\,[L\,T^{-2}]^{z} = [M]^{x}\,[L]^{y+z}\,[T]^{-2z}$. Now match the power of each base dimension on the two sides:

  • Mass: $\;0 = x \Rightarrow x = 0$ (period does not depend on mass!)
  • Time: $\;1 = -2z \Rightarrow z = -\tfrac12$
  • Length: $\;0 = y + z \Rightarrow y = \tfrac12$

So $T = k\,L^{1/2}\,g^{-1/2}$. Experiment shows the number $k = 2\pi$, giving the familiar result:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}}$$
terms
$T$
time for one full swing, in seconds s.
$L$
length of the string, in m.
$g$
acceleration due to gravity, $\approx 9.8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$.
⚠️ Limitations of dimensional analysis
  • It can never find the plain number $k$ (like $2\pi$ or $\tfrac12$).
  • It fails for equations containing $\sin$, $\cos$, $\log$ or $e^x$ (these need dimensionless inputs).
  • It can't tell you whether a quantity is a scalar or a vector.
  • It struggles when a quantity depends on more than three others.

1.5  Errors & Uncertainty

No measurement is ever perfect. A physicist's job is not to pretend otherwise, but to state honestly how uncertain a result is. First, two words that sound alike but mean different things:

📖 Error vs. uncertainty

Error is the difference between your measured value and the true value. Uncertainty is the range around your measurement inside which the true value is expected to lie — it is our honest estimate of how much we could be off. We write a result as value ± uncertainty, e.g. $(5.12 \pm 0.01)\ \mathrm{mm}$.

1.5.1  Two families of error

Systematic errorRandom error
What it isA consistent, one-direction error with a clear cause.Unpredictable scatter, different each time, often no single cause.
ExamplesA scale that reads 2 g too high; a zero error; a stopwatch running slow.Reaction-time when clicking a stopwatch; a draught nudging a balance.
DirectionAlways the same way (can be + or −, but consistent).Sometimes too high, sometimes too low.
How to reduceFind and remove the cause; calibrate the instrument.Take many readings and average them.

1.5.2  Least count & the uncertainty of a single reading

📖 Definition — Least count

The least count (or resolution) of an instrument is the smallest increment it can read — the value of its smallest scale division. A ruler marked in millimetres has a least count of $1\ \mathrm{mm}$.

When you take a single reading from an instrument, the uncertainty is half of its least count. Why half? Because you round to the nearest mark, so the true value lies within half a division either side.

🔑 Key rule

$$\text{uncertainty in one reading} = \tfrac{1}{2}\times(\text{least count})$$ Example: a bathroom scale marked every $1\ \mathrm{N}$ reads a man as $142\ \mathrm{N}$. His true weight is between $141.5$ and $142.5\ \mathrm{N}$, so we write $W = (142 \pm 0.5)\ \mathrm{N}$.

1.5.3  Fractional and percentage uncertainty

An uncertainty of $0.5\ \mathrm{N}$ is tiny on a $142\ \mathrm{N}$ weight but huge on a $1\ \mathrm{N}$ weight. To compare fairly, we scale it against the value:

$$\text{fractional uncertainty}=\frac{\Delta x}{x}, \qquad \text{percentage uncertainty}=\frac{\Delta x}{x}\times 100\%$$
terms
$x$
the measured value.
$\Delta x$
its absolute uncertainty (same units as $x$).

For the man's weight: percentage uncertainty $=\dfrac{0.5}{142}\times100\% = 0.35\%$.

1.5.4  Combining uncertainties

Usually a final answer is calculated from several measured quantities. Each one carries its own uncertainty, and they combine by three simple rules:

🔑 The three combining rules
  • Adding or subtracting quantities → add their absolute uncertainties.   If $q = a \pm b$ then $\Delta q = \Delta a + \Delta b$.
  • Multiplying or dividing quantities → add their percentage uncertainties.
  • Raising to a power $n$ → multiply the percentage uncertainty by $n$.   If $q = x^{n}$ then $\dfrac{\Delta q}{q} = n\,\dfrac{\Delta x}{x}$.
Worked example 1.2A copper wire is $18.2 \pm 0.04\ \mathrm{cm}$ long when cold and $19.7 \pm 0.02\ \mathrm{cm}$ when hot. Find its extension and the uncertainty.
Step 1 — this is a subtraction, so add the absolute uncertainties

$\Delta(\text{extension}) = 0.04 + 0.02 = 0.06\ \mathrm{cm}$.

Step 2 — subtract the values

Extension $= 19.7 - 18.2 = 1.5\ \mathrm{cm}$.

AnswerExtension $= (1.5 \pm 0.06)\ \mathrm{cm}$.
Worked example 1.3A radius is measured as $R = 7.5\ \mathrm{cm}$ with error $\Delta R = 0.03\ \mathrm{cm}$. Find the uncertainty in the sphere's volume.
Step 1 — the formula involves a cube (power 3)

$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^{3}$. Because $R$ is cubed, the percentage uncertainty is multiplied by 3:

$$\frac{\Delta V}{V} = 3\,\frac{\Delta R}{R} = 3\times\frac{0.03}{7.5} = 0.012 = 1.2\%.$$

Step 2 — find $V$, then the absolute uncertainty

$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi (7.5)^{3} = 1767\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, so $\Delta V = 0.012\times 1767 \approx 21\ \mathrm{cm^3}$.

Answer$V = (1767 \pm 21)\ \mathrm{cm^3}$, i.e. a 1.2% uncertainty.
Worked example 1.4A block's weight is $8.0 \pm 0.3\ \mathrm{N}$, resting on an area $3.5 \pm 0.2\ \mathrm{m^2}$. Find the pressure and its uncertainty.
Step 1 — pressure is a division, so add percentage uncertainties

% in weight $=\dfrac{0.3}{8.0}\times100 = 3.75\%$;   % in area $=\dfrac{0.2}{3.5}\times100 = 5.71\%$.

Total % uncertainty $= 3.75\% + 5.71\% = 9.46\%$.

Step 2 — compute pressure and convert % back to absolute

$P = \dfrac{8.0}{3.5} = 2.3\ \mathrm{Pa}$;   $\Delta P = 9.46\%\times 2.3 = 0.22\ \mathrm{Pa}$.

Answer$P = (2.3 \pm 0.2)\ \mathrm{Pa}$.

1.6  Significant Figures

📖 Definition — Significant figures

Significant figures are the digits in a number that carry real, trustworthy information about a measurement. Reporting more digits than you actually measured is fake precision.

1.6.1  The rules for counting them

RuleExampleSig. figs
1. All non-zero digits are significant.12.33
2. Zeros between non-zero digits are significant.1023
3. Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant.12.3005
4. Leading zeros (left of the first non-zero digit) are not significant.0.00562
5. A zero to the right of the decimal and of a non-zero digit is significant.0.05603

1.6.2  Using them in calculations

  • Multiplying / dividing: the answer keeps the least number of significant figures of the values used.
  • Adding / subtracting: the answer keeps the least number of decimal places.
  • Round only the final answer — never round in the middle of a calculation.

Example: a wall $2.345\ \mathrm{m}$ by $3.56\ \mathrm{m}$ has area $2.345 \times 3.56 = 8.3482\ \mathrm{m^2}$, but $3.56$ has only 3 sig figs, so we report $8.35\ \mathrm{m^2}$.

1.6.3  Scientific (exponential) notation

Very large or very small numbers are clumsy to write. In scientific notation a number is written as a coefficient (between 1 and 10) times a power of ten, $N\times10^{x}$. The speed of light $299\,792\,458\ \mathrm{m/s}$ becomes a tidy $3\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, and an electron's mass $0.000\ldots911\ \mathrm{g}$ becomes $9.11\times10^{-28}\ \mathrm{g}$. It doesn't change the value — just how we write it.

1.7  Precision, Accuracy & Resolution

These three words are used loosely in everyday life but mean very specific things in physics.

AccuracyPrecision
How close a measurement is to the true / standard value.How close repeated measurements are to each other.
Can be judged from a single measurement.Needs several measurements to judge.
You can be accurate by luck on one try.Readings can be precise yet all wrong (if there's a systematic error).
📌 Why resolution & repeating matter

An instrument with a smaller least count (higher resolution) gives more precise readings and smaller uncertainty. And taking more readings and averaging cancels out random error, revealing the true trend with more confidence. Both are cheap ways to improve an experiment.

1.8  Graphs in Physics

A graph turns a table of numbers into a picture, making trends and relationships jump out. Two terms first:

📖 Independent vs. dependent variable

The independent variable is the one you change/control (the cause); it goes on the x-axis. The dependent variable is the one you measure in response (the effect); it goes on the y-axis.

1.8.1  Line of best fit & error bars

Real data points scatter a little. The line of best fit is the single straight line that passes as close as possible to all of them, showing the overall trend. Error bars are short lines through each point showing its uncertainty — short bars mean precise data, long bars mean less precise.

1.8.2  Slope, intercept & extrapolation

For a straight-line graph we compare it with the equation of a line,

$$y = m x + c$$
terms
$m$
the slope (gradient) $= \dfrac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}$, the rise over the run.
$c$
the y-intercept — the value of $y$ where the line crosses the y-axis (when $x=0$).

For example, a position–time graph that rises by $30\ \mathrm{m}$ over $15\ \mathrm{s}$ has slope $\dfrac{30}{15} = 2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ — the slope of a position–time graph is the speed. A straight line means constant speed; a curve means the speed is changing (acceleration), and its slope is different at every point.

Extrapolation means extending the best-fit line beyond the measured data to predict a value we didn't measure. Extending a volume–temperature line down until the volume would reach zero, for instance, predicts absolute zero at $-273.15\ \mathrm{°C}$.

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

Mistake 1 — dropping the unit

A number with no unit is meaningless in physics. Always carry the unit through the whole calculation; the units on the two sides of your final equation must match.

Mistake 2 — confusing accuracy and precision

They are not the same. Tightly-grouped (precise) readings can still all be wrong (inaccurate) because of a systematic error such as a zero error.

Mistake 3 — combining uncertainties the wrong way

Add absolute uncertainties only for + / −. For × / ÷ you add percentage uncertainties, and for a power you multiply the percentage by that power. Never mix them up.

Mistake 4 — keeping too many digits

Your answer can't be more precise than your least-precise measurement. Round the final result to the correct number of significant figures.

Mistake 5 — trusting dimensions too far

A dimensionally-correct equation can still be wrong, because dimensions can't find pure numbers like $\tfrac12$ or $2\pi$. Dimensional analysis rules things out; it doesn't prove them right.

📝 Chapter Summary

Everything you must remember

  • A measurement = number + unit; the world uses the SI system, built on 7 base units (m, kg, s, A, K, mol, cd).
  • Derived units come from multiplying/dividing base units; supplementary units (rad, sr) have a unit but no dimension.
  • Dimensions $[M],[L],[T],[A],[K]$ let you (i) check equations by homogeneity and (ii) derive formulas up to a plain number.
  • Dimensional analysis can't find numbers like $2\pi$, handle $\sin/\log/e^x$, or distinguish scalars from vectors.
  • Error = measured − true; uncertainty = the ± range. Errors are systematic (consistent) or random (scattered, reduced by averaging).
  • Single reading uncertainty $=\tfrac12\times$ least count. Combining: +/− add absolute; ×/÷ add %; power $n$ → multiply % by $n$.
  • Significant figures show real precision; for ×/÷ keep the least number of sig figs, and round only at the end.
  • Accuracy = close to true value; precision = readings close to each other — a smaller least count improves precision.
  • On a graph, slope $=\Delta y/\Delta x$ and the intercept is $y$ at $x=0$; a straight position–time line means constant speed, a curve means acceleration; extrapolation extends the trend beyond the data.

Section A — Multiple Choice Questions
1. The respective numbers of significant figures for $23.023$, $0.0003$ and $2.1\times10^{-3}$ are:
  • a. 5, 1, 2
  • b. 5, 1, 5
  • c. 5, 5, 2
  • d. 4, 4, 2

$23.023$ → all 5 digits count (zeros are between non-zeros) = 5. $0.0003$ → leading zeros don't count = 1. $2.1\times10^{-3}$ → only the coefficient $2.1$ counts = 2.

Answer: a. 5, 1, 2

2. Which of the following is a supplementary quantity?
  • a. Mass
  • b. Time
  • c. Solid angle
  • d. Luminosity

Plane angle and solid angle are the two supplementary quantities (units radian and steradian).

Answer: c. Solid angle

3. The unit of solid angle is:
  • a. Second
  • b. Steradian
  • c. Kilogram
  • d. Candela

Solid angle is measured in steradians (sr).

Answer: b. Steradian

4. The quantity having the same unit in all systems of units is:
  • a. mass
  • b. time
  • c. length
  • d. temperature

The second is the unit of time in the SI, CGS and FPS systems alike — time is measured the same way everywhere.

Answer: b. time

5. Random errors can be eliminated by:
  • a. taking many observations and their mean
  • b. using more than one instrument
  • c. eliminating the cause
  • d. careful observation

Random errors scatter both ways, so averaging many readings cancels them out. (Removing a cause fixes a systematic error, not a random one.)

Answer: a. taking many observations and their mean

6. A systematic error can be:
  • a. either positive or negative
  • b. negative only
  • c. positive only
  • d. zero error

A systematic error is consistent in one direction, but that direction may make readings read too high (positive) or too low (negative) depending on the fault.

Answer: a. either positive or negative

7. $[MLT^{-2}]$ is the dimensional formula of:
  • a. strain
  • b. force
  • c. displacement
  • d. pressure

Force = mass × acceleration $=[M][LT^{-2}]=[MLT^{-2}]$. (Strain is dimensionless; displacement is $[L]$; pressure is $[ML^{-1}T^{-2}]$.)

Answer: b. force

8. Which pair has the same dimensions?
  • a. moment of inertia & torque
  • b. surface tension & force
  • c. impulse & momentum
  • d. specific heat & latent heat

Impulse $= Ft = [MLT^{-2}][T] = [MLT^{-1}]$; momentum $= mv = [M][LT^{-1}] = [MLT^{-1}]$. Identical.

Answer: c. impulse and momentum

9. The dependent variable is the:
  • a. cause
  • b. effect
  • c. cause and effect
  • d. reason

The dependent variable changes in response to the independent variable — it is the effect, plotted on the y-axis.

Answer: b. effect

10. The dimensions of kinetic energy are the same as those of:
  • a. acceleration
  • b. velocity
  • c. work
  • d. force

Kinetic energy and work are both energy, dimension $[ML^{2}T^{-2}]$.

Answer: c. work

Section B — Constructed-Response Questions (CRQs)
1.Give an example of (I) a quantity with a unit but no dimension, (II) a quantity with neither unit nor dimension, (III) a constant with a unit, (IV) a constant with no unit.

(I) Plane angle — unit radian, but dimensionless.  (II) Relative density (or strain, or refractive index) — a pure ratio, no unit and no dimension.  (III) The gravitational constant $G = 6.67\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}$.  (IV) A pure number such as $\pi$ or $2\pi$.

2.When rounding the product or quotient of two measurements, must you consider significant figures?

Yes. For multiplication or division, the answer is kept to the least number of significant figures present in the values used, because the result cannot be more precise than the least-precise measurement.

3.Derive the period of a mass on a vertical spring, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$, by dimensional analysis.

Assume $T = k'\,m^{a}k^{b}$, where the spring constant $k$ has dimension $[MT^{-2}]$ (from $F=kx$: $[MLT^{-2}]=[k][L]$). Then $[T]=[M]^{a}[MT^{-2}]^{b}=[M]^{a+b}[T]^{-2b}$. Matching: time $1=-2b\Rightarrow b=-\tfrac12$; mass $0=a+b\Rightarrow a=\tfrac12$. So $T=k'\sqrt{m/k}$, and with $k'=2\pi$, $\;T=2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.

4.Find the dimensions of (a) work, (b) energy, (c) power, (d) momentum.

(a) work = force × distance $=[ML^{2}T^{-2}]$. (b) energy — same as work $=[ML^{2}T^{-2}]$. (c) power = work ÷ time $=[ML^{2}T^{-3}]$. (d) momentum = mass × velocity $=[MLT^{-1}]$.

5.A wheel's radius is measured as $4.16\ \mathrm{cm}$. Multiplying by 2 for the diameter, should you write $8\ \mathrm{cm}$ or $8.32\ \mathrm{cm}$?

$8.32\ \mathrm{cm}$. The "2" is an exact number (a definition, not a measurement) so it has infinite significant figures. The precision is set by $4.16$ (3 sig figs), so the diameter keeps 3 sig figs: $8.32\ \mathrm{cm}$.

6.If $y = a + bt + ct^{2}$, where $y$ is in metres and $t$ in seconds, what is the unit of $c$?

Every term must have the unit of $y$ (metres). For $ct^{2}$: $[c]\times \mathrm{s^2} = \mathrm{m}$, so $[c] = \mathrm{m/s^{2}} = \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ (metre per second squared).

7.Differentiate between accuracy and precision.

Accuracy is how close a reading is to the true/standard value (needs one measurement, can happen by chance). Precision is how close repeated readings are to each other (needs several measurements). Readings can be precise without being accurate if a systematic error is present.

8.Define dependent and independent variables.

The independent variable is the one you deliberately change/control (the cause), plotted on the x-axis. The dependent variable is the one you measure in response (the effect), plotted on the y-axis.

9.Differentiate systematic error and random error.

Systematic error is consistent and in one direction, with an identifiable cause (zero error, faulty scale); it is removed by fixing the cause/calibrating. Random error scatters unpredictably in both directions; it is reduced by taking many readings and averaging.

10.List the limitations of dimensional analysis.

It cannot: find the value of a dimensionless constant (like $2\pi$); derive equations containing trigonometric, exponential or logarithmic functions; tell whether a quantity is a scalar or vector; or handle a quantity that depends on more than three others.

11.Describe the least count of a vernier calliper and a screw-gauge micrometer.

Vernier calliper: least count = (smallest main-scale division) ÷ (number of vernier divisions) $= \dfrac{1\ \mathrm{mm}}{10} = 0.1\ \mathrm{mm} = 0.01\ \mathrm{cm}$.

Screw-gauge micrometer: least count = pitch ÷ (number of circular-scale divisions) $= \dfrac{0.5\ \mathrm{mm}}{50} = 0.01\ \mathrm{mm}$. The micrometer is therefore ten times finer than the vernier.

12.Describe the method of extrapolation.

Extrapolation is extending the best-fit line or curve beyond the range of the measured data to estimate a value that was not measured. For example, extending a volume–temperature line until the volume reaches zero predicts absolute zero at $-273.15\ \mathrm{°C}$.

Section B — Extended-Response Questions (ERQs)
1.Discuss graphs and their various types with an example.

A graph is a visual display of the relationship between two variables (independent on x, dependent on y). Main types: a line graph joins points to show how one quantity changes with another (e.g. distance vs time); a bar graph compares separate categories (e.g. rainfall per month); a scatter plot shows individual data points to reveal a trend or correlation; and a best-fit line is drawn through a scatter to capture the overall trend. Example: plotting a car's position against time gives a straight line whose slope is its constant speed.

2.State the rules for determining the number of significant figures in a measurement.

(1) All non-zero digits are significant. (2) Zeros between non-zero digits are significant. (3) Trailing zeros after a decimal point are significant. (4) Leading zeros (left of the first non-zero digit) are not significant. (5) A zero right of the decimal point and right of a non-zero digit is significant. In × / ÷, keep the least number of significant figures; in + / −, keep the least number of decimal places.

3.What are the uses of dimensional analysis? Explain each.

(i) Checking an equation — both sides must have identical dimensions (homogeneity); if not, the equation is wrong. (ii) Deriving a relation — if you know which quantities a result depends on, matching powers of $[M],[L],[T]$ reconstructs the formula up to a constant (e.g. the pendulum period). (iii) Converting units — knowing the dimensions lets you convert a quantity from one system of units to another correctly.

4.A vernier calliper and a micrometer are shown in the book. Explain how each reading is taken.

Vernier: read the main-scale mark just before the zero of the vernier scale (say $1.3\ \mathrm{cm}$). Then find the vernier line that best lines up with a main-scale line (say the 4th line); multiply by the least count $0.01\ \mathrm{cm}$ to get $0.04\ \mathrm{cm}$. Reading $= 1.3 + 0.04 = 1.34\ \mathrm{cm}$.

Micrometer: read the main scale on the sleeve (say $5.5\ \mathrm{mm}$), then the circular thimble line matching the reference line (say 28) × least count $0.01\ \mathrm{mm} = 0.28\ \mathrm{mm}$. Reading $= 5.5 + 0.28 = 5.78\ \mathrm{mm}$.

Section B — Numerical Problems
1.What is the percentage uncertainty in the measurement $3.67 \pm 0.25\ \mathrm{m}$?

$\%\text{ uncertainty} = \dfrac{\Delta x}{x}\times100 = \dfrac{0.25}{3.67}\times100 = 6.81\%$.

Answer ≈ 6.8%

2.Find the area, and its approximate uncertainty, of a circle of radius $3.7\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{cm}$.

$A = \pi r^{2} = \pi(3.7\times10^{4})^{2} = 4.30\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$.

$r$ has uncertainty $\pm0.05\times10^{4}$, so $\%$ in $r = \dfrac{0.05}{3.7}\times100 = 1.35\%$. Since $A\propto r^{2}$, $\%$ in $A = 2\times1.35\% = 2.7\%$, giving $\Delta A = 0.027\times4.30\times10^{9} = 0.11\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$.

Answer: $A = (4.3 \pm 0.11)\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{cm^2}$

3.An aeroplane travels at $850\ \mathrm{km/h}$. How long does it take to travel $1.00\ \mathrm{km}$?

$t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{1.00\ \mathrm{km}}{850\ \mathrm{km/h}} = 1.176\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{h}$. Convert to seconds: $\times 3600 = 4.24\ \mathrm{s}$.

Answer ≈ 4.23 s

4.A rectangular tank is $25.0\ \mathrm{m}$ long and $15.0\ \mathrm{m}$ wide. If $2980\ \mathrm{m^3}$ of water is pumped in, what is the depth of the water?

Depth $= \dfrac{V}{L\times W} = \dfrac{2980}{25.0\times15.0} = \dfrac{2980}{375} = 7.95\ \mathrm{m}$.

Answer = 7.95 m

5.Find the volume of an underground tank measuring $1.9\ \mathrm{m}\times1.2\ \mathrm{m}\times0.8\ \mathrm{m}$.

$V = 1.9\times1.2\times0.8 = 1.824\ \mathrm{m^3}$.

Answer = 1.824 m³

6.Two students write (a) $x = v\,t^{2} + 2at$ and (b) $x = v_{0}t + 2at^{2}$. Which is dimensionally possible?

$x$ has dimension $[L]$; each term must match.

(a) $v\,t^{2} = [LT^{-1}][T^{2}] = [LT]$ — not $[L]$. So (a) is dimensionally incorrect.

(b) $v_{0}t = [LT^{-1}][T] = [L]$ ✓ and $at^{2} = [LT^{-2}][T^{2}] = [L]$ ✓. So (b) is dimensionally correct.

Answer: (a) incorrect, (b) correct

7.One hectare $= 10^{4}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ and one acre $= 4\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{ft^2}$. How many acres are in one hectare? (Take $1\ \mathrm{m} = 3.28\ \mathrm{ft}$.)

$1\ \mathrm{m^2} = (3.28)^{2}\ \mathrm{ft^2} = 10.76\ \mathrm{ft^2}$, so $1\ \text{hectare} = 10^{4}\times10.76 = 1.076\times10^{5}\ \mathrm{ft^2}$.

Acres $= \dfrac{1.076\times10^{5}}{4\times10^{4}} = 2.69\ \text{acres}$.

Answer ≈ 2.69 acres

8.A watch gains or loses no more than $10\ \mathrm{s}$ in a year. Express its accuracy as a percentage.

Seconds in a year $= 365\times24\times3600 = 3.15\times10^{7}\ \mathrm{s}$.

Accuracy $= \dfrac{10}{3.15\times10^{7}}\times100 = 3.16\times10^{-5}\%$.

Answer ≈ 3.16 × 10⁻⁵ %

9.The Moon's diameter is $3480\ \mathrm{km}$. Find the Moon's volume, and how many Moons equal the Earth's volume. (Earth radius $= 6380\ \mathrm{km}$.)

Moon radius $= 1740\ \mathrm{km} = 1.74\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$.  $V_{\text{moon}} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi(1.74\times10^{6})^{3} = 2.2\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{m^3}$.

Earth radius $= 6.38\times10^{6}\ \mathrm{m}$.  $V_{\text{earth}} = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi(6.38\times10^{6})^{3} = 1.09\times10^{21}\ \mathrm{m^3}$.

Number of Moons $= \dfrac{V_{\text{earth}}}{V_{\text{moon}}} = \dfrac{1.09\times10^{21}}{2.2\times10^{19}} \approx 49.3$.

Answer: $V_{\text{moon}} \approx 2.2\times10^{19}\ \mathrm{m^3}$; about 49.3 Moons
